Our tone of voice principles

In the same way customers can spot HSBC from our colour palette and logo, they should be able to recognise us by how we write and speak, too. That's where our tone of voice comes in.

Summary

We have four principles that make up our tone of voice:
- Human
- Insightful
- Confident
- Intelligent wit

Together, they communicate the values and qualities that are most important to us as a business.

== HUMAN ==

We're a big bank, but we still want to sound personal. Because we know the best way to succeed is together, across boundaries. Our writing style is down-to-earth and human, so our audience feels like we're on the same level.

Say 'we' and 'you'
Example: 'We're writing to you about your current account' sounds much warmer and more personal than: 'Today HSBC is communicating to all current account customers'

Use contractions
Contractions also warm up our tone, by making our writing sound more conversational and human. Short contractions like 'I'm', 'we'll' and 'you're' are great. We avoid longer, clunky ones like 'wouldn't've' or 'shouldn't've'. Occasionally it makes sense to write the words out in full for effect. For example, to emphasise that 'I will' do something as promised. But generally, we use contractions wherever we can.

Avoid formal language
We avoid using formal terms that make us seem distant or like we're talking down to our audience. Even if formality varies across markets, it's best to keep your writing personal. Opt for natural language that's closer to how you might speak.

Formal → Human table:
Advise, inform → Tell
Assist → Help
Cease → Stop
Continue → Carry on
Converse → Talk
Endeavour → Try
Enquire, request → Ask
Ensure → Make sure
Funds → Money
Obtain, receive → Get
Provide → Give
Require → Need
Therefore → So

Make sure it's readable
'Readability' is how easily our writing can be understood. We use plain English and simple sentences. We use familiar words, instead of financial or legal jargon, wherever we can. This doesn't just make our writing easier on the eye and better suited to mobile devices, writing that's readable is also more inclusive. Many factors might affect someone's reading ability – like their age, education, whether English is their first language, and whether they're using text readers. Better readability also supports people with neurodiverse conditions like attention deficit disorder (ADD), autism, dyslexia and dyspraxia.

Readability checkers like the Flesch Reading Ease Score or Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level can help. There's a built-in checker in Microsoft Word (File > Options > Proofing > 'Show readability statistics').

**All our writing should have a Flesch-Kincaid score of 70+ (no higher than level 7, which is the reading age of 11 year olds).** This is around the readability of the 'Harry Potter' books. In comparison, 'Time' magazine typically scores around grade 12, and the 'Harvard Law Review' is at graduate student level.

Sometimes, we'll need to be flexible and the score to aim for will change depending on what you're writing. In any case, we should make every effort to bring our scores as close to 70+ as possible.

Type of writing → Score to aim for:
Internal email 60-70+
Customer email 60-70+
Customer letter 60+
Social media 70+
Report 50+
Technical report 45+
Terms and conditions 40+

To boost your readability score, try simplifying your language and shortening your sentences.
[Worked example: business-banking letter rewrite — complex/cold/long-sentence version vs plain rewrite. Before: "From 1 April 2021 it will no longer be necessary for cardholders to sign individual agreements with HSBC. We will continue to ask cardholders each month to pay for card spend and, as now, will only revert to the company when payment is not made." After: "You won't have to sign individual agreements with us after 1st April 2021. You'll still need to pay your bill every month, and the company is still ultimately responsible if you don't."]

Use the active voice
Writing in the active voice means it's clear who's doing what in a sentence. But in the passive voice the people disappear. Example: "A closing statement and tax pack will be sent in due course." (passive) vs "We'll send you a closing statement and tax pack in due course." (active). Using the active voice forces us to talk about people, not banking processes, making us sound more warm and human right away.

== INSIGHTFUL ==

We're always aware that what we do has a real impact on people's lives. We take this responsibility seriously, so we structure our writing in a way that shows we understand how our reader might feel and what's important to them.

Put your main point first
We're conscious of our audience's time. So we put our most important information first, and only tell them what they need to know. This means we don't waffle, write in long sentences, or use lots of words when just a few will do.
[Worked example: Private Banking Avaloq IT-platform letter — buried point vs "We're making some changes to how we use your data" lead.]
Be aware that what you think the key point is and what a reader really needs to know aren't always the same thing. So get clear on what will matter to them before you start writing.

Use summary subheadings to highlight what's important
So that important or time-sensitive information isn't missed or ignored, we use summary subheadings to draw attention to key points. This helps readers who are short on time – they can skim the subheadings and still take away all the most important information.
[Worked example: PAD Certification message with deadline subheadings.]
Subheadings are also vital to making your writing more accessible online, especially on websites, as they break up text so it's easier for readers to skim and digest. Customers with visual impairments, or neurodiverse conditions such as dyslexia or autism, often find it easier to read text that's presented in this way. Subheadings benefit readers who access webpages in other ways too, by helping screen-readers or text-to-speech software navigate between paragraphs and webpage content more easily.
[Page cross-links to the neurodiversity design guidelines.]

Explain why something is changing
Showing that we understand our reader is important when a change is taking place – especially if we're giving them unwelcome news. So if something is changing, we always tell them why.
[Worked example: delayed Premier Investment Management statement, COVID postal-team explanation.]

== CONFIDENT ==

We're known for our confident approach to business. We make clear choices and take bold actions. Our writing should reflect the confidence our brand is known for in the financial world. How this looks might differ between markets, as perceptions of confidence can vary. So, when applying this principle, it's a good idea to sense check it with a local colleague, to avoid appearing arrogant.

Avoid vague language
Vague language like 'we're committed to' or 'we endeavour to' is a common pitfall of financial writing. It creates distance between us and the action and makes us appear unreliable and non-committal. Instead, we should use direct language that says what we're going to do.
Example: "We're committed to protecting you and all our customers from the growing risks of fraud and financial crime." → "We're asking you for this information to protect you and all our customers from fraud and financial crime."

Always say what happens next
People always feel more confident when they know what to expect. So, whenever we're sharing information, particularly about something new or different, we tell our reader what happens next.
Example: "Thanks for updating your medical insurance details. Your level of cover has now been updated. You'll get a letter in the post to confirm what's changed, and any additional tax deductions will automatically show up on your next payslip."
**Even if no more action is needed, be sure to say that clearly.**

== INTELLIGENT WIT ==

We take time to learn new perspectives as well as offer them. So we get to know the world of our reader, even if it's different from our own. And we include intelligent or witty details that give a knowing nod to who they are, or where they happen to be.

**Wit isn't needed – or appropriate – everywhere. We're always tactful in difficult situations (such as pandemic and bereavement), and we shouldn't ever distract from important messages that need to be clear and straightforward. Use your judgement to decide how far to go, depending on what you have to say.**

Use specific examples
No two audiences are the same. Show you're thinking intelligently about your reader, by using specific references that will resonate with them. [Worked examples: ultra-high-net-worth legacy framing; HSBC Life Retirement Income; UAE out-of-home campaign with locally specific headlines.]

Give a fresh perspective
We avoid clichés or stock expressions that make us sound like everyone else. Instead, we offer a fresh perspective that reflects our diversity of thought.
[Worked example: "24 hours a day, 7 days a week" = cliché any bank could use; vs "Today, shed office. Tomorrow, head office." Tomorrow Ready programme poster.]
There's room to bring in a fresh perspective and intelligent or witty observations into everything we write, from simple service messages to good news moments. **In a more functional message, it's better to keep the wit quite subtle.** Like: 'Next thing you know, you're banking on your way to brunch.' While in advertising or marketing messages, it's possible to dial this up much further ['We Are Not An Island' campaign posters]. [LinkedIn accessibility post example: "Link Carrot Dash Down" simulating screen-reader audio.]

Mix in colloquialisms
We show our local expertise by using natural, everyday expressions that our audience will know and connect with. Examples: 'You're cut from a different cloth' (Leeds textile-city poster) · 'Just go online, give us a call or pop into a branch' (student account webpage) · 'Did you catch our CEO on Bloomberg TV this morning?' (LinkedIn) · 'Let's make a start on your future' (savings brochure) · 'Our research shows reforming the EU to make it more competitive is less risky than going it alone' (corporate investor letter).

If you know your writing will be going into several languages, use a 'transcreation' service. It's more than just a straight translation – they'll make sure these expressions and our tone of voice land well. You can also reach out to colleagues in that market for a local sense check. If you're ever working to translate colloquialisms and are having trouble, it's best to keep things simple. We don't want the clarity of our message to get lost in translation.

**You should also consider that neurodiverse readers may interpret language very literally. So stick to clear, unambiguous phrases in functional messages, or when telling readers to take an action.**

== SUMMARY (at a glance) ==

1 Human — Stay personal and down to earth, and speak to your reader as an equal.
  (Say 'we' and 'you' · Use contractions · Avoid formal language · Make sure it's readable · Use the active voice)
2 Insightful — Be aware of the impact we have on people's everyday lives.
  (Put your main point first · Use summary subheadings · Explain why something is changing)
3 Confident — Make sure our confident approach to business is reflected in the way that you write.
  (Avoid vague language · Always say what happens next)
4 Intelligent wit — Use intelligent and witty observations to make your writing reflect your reader's world.
  (Use specific examples · Give a fresh perspective · Mix in colloquialisms)
