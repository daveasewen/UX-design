== FAQ — the 'what' and the 'why' ==

What is tone of voice? It's how we write and speak as HSBC. How our brand comes to life in words. Tone is the way we express ourselves: our word choice, phrasing, structure. It covers how we write to both customers and colleagues.

Why do we need it? Because it's better for our customers, and better for us. When we speak with one voice that's true to who we are, we show the world – and each other – what we stand for. Customers feel valued, we stand out from competitors, and we work better together.

Do I write as myself or the brand? Think: who's 'signing' your work? If it's coming from HSBC – our website, social media, brochures, terms and conditions – **write as HSBC**. If it's an email to customers signed by a person but on behalf of HSBC (like a mass email update) – write as the brand. If it's coming from you personally (emails with colleagues) – write as yourself, but feel free to use the tips you've learned.

I'm not a writer. Can I skip this? If you write at all, you need to know about tone of voice. You're still using words to represent the brand.

== FAQ — the ins and outs of our voice ==

What does 'insightful' mean in practice? Focusing on what's important; considering your audience before you start writing; seeing the bigger picture of what's going on in our customers' lives.

I'm not funny, how do I do the whole 'Intelligent wit' thing? **Of our four principles, it's the trickiest to master.** Think of intelligent wit as the kind of wry observation or clever aside that gets a knowing smile, rather than a laugh. **It's more of a flourish than something we use throughout – it works best in headlines or when we're giving good news.**

How do I greet and how do I sign off? In short: 'Dear' for letters. 'Hello' or 'Hi' for emails and texts. 'Yours sincerely' for letters, 'Thank you' or 'Thanks' for emails and texts. Think about your audience (guidance differs by customer segment) and be mindful of the cultural expectation.

Can I use contractions? Is 'we'll' okay? Definitely use contractions. **Just keep it to a 'single' contraction: I'll, we'll, can't, won't, don't. 'Double' contractions like 'shouldn't've' feel harder to follow on the page, so we stay away from them.**

Do you have a bank of preferred terms? Yes — see the style guide. It shows which terms we use at HSBC, **like ATM (rather than cash machine), or every two weeks (rather than fortnightly)**.

Scales of formality (from the formality guidance + video summary):
**In every language there's a scale of formality which stretches from informal slang to formal and old-fashioned. Our tone of voice doesn't shift to either of these extremes, it sits in the middle so we can easily adapt to different markets.** For example, Australia is probably our most informal market. France and Argentina are more traditional, using 'vous' or 'vos' instead of the more familiar 'tu'. In India and Malaysia, social hierarchy is very important, meaning our tone will shift a few degrees towards the more formal end of the spectrum. **Wherever in the world you're writing for, your priority should always be making sure your writing is clear and customers can easily understand it.** Take into account local spelling conventions or terminology ('cheque' UK vs 'check' US; 'Secure Key' UK vs 'Security Device' Hong Kong). **Watch out for idioms. Be careful not to use phrases that non-native speakers might not immediately understand. For example, instead of 'get the ball rolling' simply say, 'get started'.**

== FAQ — worries ==

Have legal agreed that we can write like this? **Yes. Not only have they agreed that we can write like this, they prefer when we do.** Legal want us to be as clear and concise as possible; we run into trouble when we cloud our meaning with jargon, passive sentences, and dense paragraphs.

What if I don't sound professional when I write using a conversational tone? Professional communications are ones that our customers can understand – the first time they read them.

If we keep it short, won't we sound rude? Not if we're telling people what they need to know. We sound rude or abrupt when we're missing information – for example, saying 'No' with no further explanation.

Why are you saying to lead with the bad news? **Science has shown that people don't want to dig for bad news. They prefer it when you tell them and then explain the rationale, and what you'll do next. Otherwise you risk sounding defensive. Or worse, your readers will miss the point altogether.**
