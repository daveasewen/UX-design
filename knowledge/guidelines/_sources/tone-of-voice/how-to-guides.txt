How-to guides

We've divided these guides into broad categories. Some communications might cross over more than one category, so mix and match. To decide which guide to use: think about it from the customer's point of view (good news, bad news or just information?) and from our point of view (what do we want them to know, feel and do?).

== 1. How to give unwelcome news ==
(branch closing, savings rate reducing, new/higher charges, product withdrawal)
Our tone of voice wasn't only designed for happier times. When it comes to unwelcome news, how we sound becomes more important than ever.

Stay warm and human — some writers fall back into a more formal, stiff style; there's a misconception that this is the polite or respectful way to write. But formality affects readability and harms the customer experience. So:
- Say 'we' and 'you'
- Use contractions
- Swap formal words for human ones
- **Keep your readability score at grade 7 or below.**
- Use the active voice
- Don't try to make bad news sound like good news, or force in a positive message where there isn't one
[Worked example: account-closure letter "I refer to our letter dated <date>… I regret to advise you…" → "We wrote to you on <date> to ask for your identification. Because we've not received it, we now need to close your HSBC bank account."]

Use the right channel — it might be best to phone rather than write; have a draft response prepared before the call; follow up in writing so the recipient has a concrete record.

Say sorry if you need to — When we've done something wrong, we say sorry. Just like that. We don't go over the top. **Say sorry once, concisely, near the beginning of your message. But don't say sorry or make excuses for things that are beyond our control, like regulatory or market changes.**
Formal, insincere-sounding apologies to avoid: "We apologise for any inconvenience caused." / "Please accept our sincerest apologies." / "Regretfully, mistakes were made…"
In line with our tone: "We're sorry." / "We're sorry for the mistake." / "I'm sorry about what happened."

Don't be vague, get to the point quickly — being direct and honest; taking too long to get to the point can appear untrustworthy. Watch out for throat-clearing phrases: "It has come to our attention…" / "Following a recent review…" / "As you'll be aware…" (often a sign of unnecessary background). However, if it's very bad news, it might feel too abrupt to come straight out with it — you can prepare the reader: "We wanted to let you know that the fees on your account will be increasing from 1st January." / "We're writing to let you know that your savings rate will be changing from June." / "Unfortunately, we've found fraudulent activity on your account." / "Your local branch is closing soon – we'll help you find other ways to bank." Depending on the style of the communication, it might even be appropriate to include this main message in the document heading or subject line.

Signpost options and solutions and let them know what happens next — consider all the possible options the reader might want to take next, then outline them clearly. Tell the customer when the change takes effect, what impact it has, what to do with questions. Suggest how we can help (phone number, more info online). If increasing a fee, remind them of accounts they could switch to; if closing a branch, tell them how to find alternatives. Make this advice prominent (own paragraph with subheading, a Johnson box, or highlighted digitally). Don't end abruptly — sign off warmly and give options to get in touch: "If you have any questions or would like to talk to someone, call [xx]." / "We're here if you'd like to talk through what your options are. Start a chat or call us."

Tell them why — clear reasons why the change is happening. **Keep it concise and straightforward: any more than a couple of sentences is too much detail.** No banking-regulation detail or internal processes. Avoid hedging: "we're committed to protecting…" → "We're asking you for this information to protect you and all our customers from fraud and financial crime." [Also: long Private Bank Suisse terms-change paragraph → "New regulations over the last few years mean that it costs us more to offer this service to you. So that we don't compromise on quality, we're going to have to change our terms for clients with balances below USD 3 million, and we'll no longer be able to offer special pricing."]

== 2. How to communicate sensitive subjects ==
(bereavement, insolvency, joint account closed in a divorce)

Pay attention to the customer journey — don't make them repeat information they've already given us or fill in a form that doesn't work. Think about the surrounding steps in the journey, not just the one message. If you find clunky language, put it right; if fixing language reveals gaps in the process itself, escalate properly.

Avoid euphemisms — expressions like 'passed away' or 'moved on' are milder substitutes for difficult ideas, but it's better to speak more plainly. We want to come across as understanding and helpful, without being overly sentimental – this can sound insincere coming from a bank. **Euphemisms can also cause real difficulties for people with neurodiverse conditions or different levels of reading ability.**
Appropriate: "We're sorry to hear that Mr [name] has died." / "We just wanted to update on what's happening with Mrs [name]'s bank accounts."
No need for 'your late husband' or the needlessly formal 'the deceased'. We can use the person's name.

Give crystal clear next steps — make main points, give clear next steps, keep messages concise. Subheading examples for dissolving a joint account: "Your joint accounts will close on [date]" / "If there's any money left in the account, we'll send you a cheque" / "We suggest you look at the regular payments you have set up". Give clear contact details; a single point of contact (even a team) helps.

== 3. How to write internal communications ==
Same standards as customer-facing. Covers leader updates, benefits brochures, restructure comms.

Help people save time — short, clear, consistent messages. **Smart Brevity** formula (built by journalists): prioritise essential news, explain its impact, deliver concisely and visually. Ask: main point first? compact story with subheadings/bolding/bullets? clear next steps? anything to cut? **Start every update with "What's new" and "Why it matters".** Subject-line example: "Quick update on team dinner / night out" → "Tell me your menu choices for the team dinner by tomorrow".

Write like you'd sound in person — **updates should be under 300 words and paragraphs in 1-2 sentences** – keeping text scannable and preventing reader fatigue. Turn the formality down a notch (or several). If writing for a senior leader, write in words they'd really use (sentence length, asides, pop culture references, regional dialect).

Avoid corporate jargon and say what you mean — 'values-led high performance culture' → 'a culture where performance and values matter most'. [Talent-pipeline worked example.] Phrases like 'we are committed to' or 'we aim to' sound hesitant: "The Board is committed to prioritising further investment in systems and controls." → "We're ready to invest more in systems and controls."

Avoid passive sentences — "A further 100,000 employees are expected to receive this training in 2022." → "In 2022, we expect to train another 100,000 people."

Break the story up and use helpful subheadings — people rarely read reports cover to cover., [sic] different sections read by different audiences. 'Outlook' tells the reader little; 'Tougher markets, tighter margins' tells more. **As a general rule, keep your sentences under 25 words** and break up long ones. Mix longer and shorter sentences for emphasis. Bullet points break up bulky paragraphs (especially long lists of figures). **A subheading every three to four paragraphs** makes longer sections feel less dense.

Know your audience — filter out anything the reader already knows; focus on the most significant, urgent or actionable information. For divisive news (e.g. working-from-home policy change), keep the tone neutral, factual and clear. At the end, say where questions and feedback go.

Make it visually appealing — clean, elegant templates, optimised white space, line breaks and imagery; use our branded templates.

== 4. How to communicate good news ==
(rate going up, charges going down, positive returns)
Customers often don't read letters/emails from their bank, even good news. Subheadings let them skim; white space makes writing look more readable.

Get to the point — summarise the main point in the first line, then extra detail in order of priority.
Let the benefit speak for itself — **avoid phrases like 'you'll be delighted to know', and words like 'amazing', 'fantastic' and 'brilliant'. They sound salesy and presumptuous.** Make clear how the change benefits them.
Give a clear call to action — crystal clear what, when and how. **Topping and tailing your writing with a clear call to action** makes the customer more likely to act. "Visit our website. Sign and send back a document. Call us."
Put a twist on a headline — if right for the situation: "A Small Business Loan could give your business the money it needs." → "We see the potential, too." / "Apply for an HSBC Premier Current Account" → "HSBC Premier focuses on the most important economy in the world. Yours." Use your judgement – you know your audience best.

== 5. How to get people to take action ==
(mandatory training, confirm identity, update contact information)

Keep it simple and make it easy — clear, straightforward, accessible language; first person; simple but not simplistic; active not passive. Make it really clear what they need to do; focus on the benefit; make it personal and relevant. **Keep to one message — don't bombard with multiple offers or recommendations in one communication.**
Signpost clearly — headings like 'What to do now' draw the eye to the call to action.
Open and close with a call to action — CTA as close to the beginning as possible, then again towards the end, or in a PS if appropriate. This can improve response rates.
Tell them why — clear rationale, exactly what to do and the date to do it by. Send them to the right place: don't automatically give a phone number; consider a link to a specific page (not the general site). For letters, a short, memorable, easy-to-type URL. If suggesting a call, put the number and opening hours in the main body, not small print. **Generally, we should avoid using asterisks and small print as much as possible.**
Explain what might happen if they don't act — no threats, just facts: "If we don't get your application form back by 1st June, we won't be able to offer you our special rates."
Avoid the hard sell — words and phrases to avoid:
- Subjective adjectives like brilliant, fantastic, amazing
- Simply (as in 'simply visit our website')
- Did you know…?
- Remember or don't forget
- Hurry!
- Free!
- Dear Valued Customer

== 6. How to guide people through a process ==
Lists of instructions can feel like a series of commands — add warmth. Use 'You'll need to' and 'It's important that you' to soften imperatives. **Add warmth to form fields with personal pronouns: 'Your details' not 'Account holder's details'; 'Please tell us' not 'Please indicate'.**
Order the steps logically — imagine the customer's questions in order ('What's this? What does it do? Should I switch it on?…') and answer in that order. [Secure Key activation worked example.]
Explain where they are in the process — "You've set up your username and password. Now we just need you to give us some security details, then you'll be able to get started."
Signpost the call to action clearly — subheading like 'To activate your account' or 'What to do next'.

== 7. How to write forms and attachments ==
Use the first person — address enclosures to a specific individual; write as a person, not the whole organisation. "HSBC have changed their terms and conditions, and that affects certain customers." → "We've changed our terms and conditions, and it might affect you." Sign off with your number, not a general helpline, if you can.
Active, not passive — "This essential information is required for a UK address." → "You need to fill in this information if you live in the UK."
Limit how much you send and say — **keep to two enclosures at a time**; consider a webpage for more. Extra information detracts from the main message.
Get to the point — don't make readers wade through information they don't need or hunt for the relevant section.
Clearly label the variable parts — give page numbers/sections ("summary on page 3", "if you ticked yes, jump to section 3B"); refer to each enclosure correctly with clear headings.
Make it easy to use — long attachments get a summary paragraph at the top. **Don't ask customers to fill in details we already know — pre-populate as much as we can.** Enough space for every field; **boxes instead of lines** for legibility. Email attachments increase message size and spam-filter risk. Always give a way to find out more. If they must return something, say it at the top and include an addressed envelope (preferably pre-paid). For multiple returns, give a checklist (perhaps on the envelope): "Check you've included: your completed form, your signed declaration, your cheque or Direct Debit slip."
Bring out the benefits — "Please use capital letters to fill out this form." → "Please use capital letters so we can read your form easily. That will make it quicker for us to process – so we'll be able to get back to you with a decision sooner."

== 8. How to write for different channels ==
**Our voice doesn't change, regardless of whether we're writing a letter, email, text or tweet.** Think about which channel works best for the message (T&C change = letter; mortgage-application acknowledgement = text). Make sure writing works on desktops, mobiles and tablets: **avoid platform-specific language like 'click' ('select' works on all devices), and don't describe the visual appearance of user interfaces.** Optimise for search engines. [Cross-reference: Digital Copy Guide for more detail.]

Emails:
- Make them want to open it — the why in the subject line, **fewer than eight words** ("You've got some of your ISA allowance left" / "We're updating your Premier account"). **Avoid spam-filter trigger words: 'Free', 'Email', 'Buy', 'Cash', '100%' and 'Income'.**
- **Stick to the two-scroll rule** — reader's probably on a smartphone; more than two scrolls = too long. Multiple messages → separate emails.
- Tell them what to do next — **opening and closing your email with a call to action** serves both immediate clickers and full readers.

Letters:
- Start with a heading — summarise the reason for writing, as succinctly as possible.
- Think about who should sign it — a specific person prepared to respond directly (e.g. relationship manager), else a team.

Tweets:
- Know the limits — 280 characters is a limit, not a target.
- Have a conversation — no stock responses; write each tweet from scratch; refer to people by real name if known.
- Treat customer enquiries with care — always be specific about what happens next (who's coming back to them, which team it's passed to).
- Be clear and concise — 'if' instead of 'in the event that…'; avoid corporate jargon and overly formal bank language.
[Cross-reference: Global Marketing Social Media Standards, staff only.]

Texts:
- Be a brutal editor — every word counts; don't repeat things already said or done.
- Explain why, if there's space — asking someone to contact their branch without saying why can cause panic.
- **Ask people to do one thing at a time** — not the medium for complex messages; wait until stage one is complete, then send the next, or move to email.

Web pages and PDFs:
- People read differently online — F-shape scanning; eyes drawn to top left and right first, then skim the left-hand side; bottom right least read.
- **Shorten your sentences — as a rule of thumb, keep them under 15 words with a maximum of three sentences per paragraph.**
- Make it skimmable — short paragraphs, more subheadings than in a letter; subheadings that summarise the paragraph below work best (readers can skip inapplicable content).

== 9. Greetings and sign offs ==
Subtle but significant; varies by line of business. **Unless the person signing off can respond to customers directly, use 'Your HSBC X Team' (e.g. 'Your HSBC Advance Team') rather than an individual person.** **Always address customers by name; if not possible (system limitations / name unknown), drop the salutation completely.**

Greetings:
- Private Banking: Letter 'Dear [preferred name]' · Text 'Dear [preferred name]' · Email 'Hello'
- Commercial Banking and GBM: Letter 'Dear [preferred name]' · Text 'Dear [preferred name]' · Email 'Hello'
- Retail: Letter 'Dear [first name]' · Text 'Hi [first name]' · Email 'Hello'

Sign offs:
- Private Banking: Letter 'Yours sincerely' · Text 'Thank you' · Email 'Thank you'
- Commercial Banking and GBM: Letter 'Yours sincerely' · Text 'Thank you' · Email 'Thank you'
- Retail: Letter 'Yours sincerely' · Text 'Thanks or thank you' · Email 'Thanks'
