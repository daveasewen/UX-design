Editorial style guide
This guide provides advice on spelling, grammar and formatting.

About this guide
This guide helps to make sure that our writing is accurate, consistent and effective. It also helps customers to make the right financial decisions and protects the bank from reputational and regulatory risks.
The bank’s Global Content discipline is responsible for maintaining and updating this guide. If you are a writer and would like to propose an update to this guide, please email customer.design.team@hsbc.com.

Writing for digital copy: special considerations
Writing digital copy requires specific style considerations. This is because digital copy is experienced differently. For example, readers like to scan through digital copy quickly and may be using a device with a small screen. Financial copy can also often be transactional in nature, so writing needs to be clear, concise, helpful and empathetic.

Accessibility
Good digital writing plays an important role in making our public websites, apps and internal systems accessible and usable by all customers and staff.
We consider accessibility in the guidance provided in this style guide. If you'd like further information on writing for accessibility, see the accessibility guidelines.

A common language?
We use UK English as our global standard. This means –ise not –ize, colour not color, fulfil not fulfill and instalment not installment.
Avoid using idiomatic, local phrases that may not be understood elsewhere in the world. This recognises the global nature of our business and customers. It also makes our content more accessible and helps those using English as a second language.
For queries on spelling, please refer to the Oxford Dictionary of English or the English Oxford Dictionaries Online.
If you’re working in the US and are publishing local content solely for a local audience, use US English.

Plain English
Our style supports the aims of our tone of voice in expressing HSBC as human, insightful, confident and using intelligent wit where appropriate.
Wherever possible, always use simple words or phrases that will be widely recognised. Avoid slang and jargon. Keep sentences and paragraphs short.

Abbreviations
Acronyms, initialisms and other abbreviations
Abbreviations can be:
Initialisms, made up of the initials of a phrase or organisation, but pronounced as separate letters, such as HSBC or BBC News
Acronyms, where those initials are pronounced as a word, such as NATO
Shortened words, such as ‘photo’ for ‘photograph’
We write acronyms and initialisms all in capitals with no full stops or spaces between the letters.
Where an initialism is better known than its full name, you can use it without writing the full name, for example, PDF.
In all other cases, the first time you use a lesser-known acronym, write the term using whole words, then put the short version in brackets immediately afterwards.
For example:
Your ticket to a world of opportunities in the Greater Bay Area (GBA).
Take care to use the correct indefinite article before initialisms beginning with certain letters. It should be 'an' rather than 'a' if the first letter is F, H, L, M, N, R, S or X, even though these are consonants. That's because the names for these letters are actually pronounced as 'ef', 'aitch' etc, which all begin with a vowel.
For example, it should be 'an NI number' not 'a NI number' even though when written in full it would be 'a National Insurance number'.
After you’ve written it out in full once, it may be appropriate to use the short version to refer to it for the rest of the page or document. However, consider carefully, as we shouldn’t expect customers to remember acronyms when scanning pages quickly, or completing multi-page journeys. HSBC usability and accessibility testing supports this.
For abbreviations of Latin phrases, such as ie, eg, etc, use lower case letters without spaces or full stops between the letters.

Active and passive
Write active sentences, not passive ones. For example, say “The teacher taught the students”, not “The students were taught by the teacher”. Active sentences are easier to read as it’s clear who has done what.
Passive sentences do have the benefit of not assigning the ‘do-er’ of the statement (ie there is no one ‘doing’ the action). This can be useful but should be used rarely.

Alt text
Alt text is needed if an image is more than simply decorative.
Ask yourself this: "does the copy make sense without the image?" If the answer is yes, then no alt text is needed.
Examples of images that do need alt text are:
graphs
QR codes
screenshots

Bold text
Bold text should be used sparingly – and try to avoid its use within body copy. Reasons include:
Accessibility can be negatively impacted as most screen readers don’t distinguish between bold and regular text - so any meaning conveyed in this way may not be shared with customers (which can expose the bank to risks)
Large amounts of bold text can confuse and distract some readers. Further details in our neurodiversity guidelines
Over-use of bold can encourage the reader to skip a section of text rather than focus on it
There are other ways to highlight text without the use of bold:
Use single quotation marks where appropriate, as shown in the ‘Punctuation’ section in this style guide
Front load sentences by putting the key information first, such as what it’s about or who it’s for - for example: ‘Premier customers get a choice of 2 exclusive credit cards’
Use headings, bullet points, steps and visual design enhancements to make pages easier to navigate and read

Bullet points (includes numbered and tick lists)
Bullet points can be a useful way to make writing easier for our customers to read. Our approach to punctuating lists focuses on:
Being suitable for short and longer forms of digital writing
Minimising punctuation to help readers scan text
Helping writers to easily format bullet points consistently
Bullet points should always begin with a capital letter and not have a full stop at the end. Example 1 features a list of items – don't use more than one sentence per bullet, use commas or dashes instead, or separate into two bullet points. Example 2 features a list of longer bullet points.
Example 1
To apply for this account, you’ll need one of the following:
Driving licence
Passport
National ID card
Example 2
Important information about repaying your loan:
If you repay early, there may be a reduction in the amount of interest you'll have to pay
Once you've given us notice, we'll give you a settlement figure - you’ll need to pay the full amount owed to close the loan
There is a charge of one month's interest for repaying your loan early
A list of links
On some of our web and app pages, bullets have been added as more of a design feature but aren’t really needed – for example, a table of contents in which each is a link anchoring to the relevant section of the page.
Cancel a standing order
Amend a standing order
Set up a standing order
Numbered steps
Use numbered steps when a customer needs to read a list in a specific order, for example a step-by-step guide to activating a card:
Log on to online banking
Select the account you want to activate the card for
Select 'Manage', then 'Activate card'
Green ticks
These are generally used to highlight benefits or features – there are two ways they can be used:
Green ticks with a bold heading and separate copy underneath (eg website product page benefits list). Heading starts with a capital letter and does not have a full stop. Body copy starts with a capital letter and has a full stop.
Green ticks without a heading (eg listing page benefits list). Copy starts with a capital letter and does not have a full stop

Buttons
Well written button label copy plays an important part in creating intuitive, delightful digital customer experiences. Copy which is unclear can confuse and create anxiety about what happens next. In extreme cases, it risks customers making the wrong financial decisions, which in turn exposes the bank to risks such as regulatory and reputational risk.
Do:
Use sentence case and no full stops
Be concise
Use action verbs to inspire users to act (for example Save, Apply, Send)
Be precise in what action they are performing (for example, ‘Cancel’ rather than ‘Delete’ if cancelling a recurring payment)
Use HSBC-preferred terms, avoiding generic terms like ‘Find out more’ or ‘Click here’
In marketing contexts, communicate the value of selecting the button
Avoid the use of jargon
Accessibility
Buttons always have alt text matching the button label, ensuring they can be read by screen readers.

Capital letters
To use capitals or not to use capitals? It’s worth taking the time to get this right. It helps make things clear for the reader and helps us sound consistently human and on tone. And take a look at our Preferred Terms list, which has specific examples.
When we capitalise
The first word of sentences and headings.
Like this sentence. It’s called sentence case and makes things quick and instinctive for your reader.
Proper nouns
Names of specific people, places, nationalities, languages, job titles, publications, and HSBC products and services (with or without HSBC in front of them).
For example, the capitalised words in the following sentence are proper nouns because they name a specific person, role, product and service:
Jane Smith, the Mayor of London, loves the HSBC Rewards Credit Card and using HSBC Online Banking.
If a proper noun has words like ‘a’, ‘of’, ‘and’ in it, we leave those lower case, for example: Head of Digital.
When we don’t capitalise
Common nouns
Generic names and places. Job titles when not referring to a specific individual or role. Anything that’s not a proper noun or brand / product name.
For example, in the next sentence, ‘mayors’, ‘credit cards’ and ‘online banking’ are common nouns because they’re talking generally:
We’ve heard that mayors from around the country love using credit cards and online banking.
Try to avoid using too many product names and opt for common nouns where you can.
For example:
You can use our app to manage your account on the go.
Not:
You can use the HSBC Mobile Banking app to manage your account.
After colons or semi-colons
You’re not starting a new sentence, so there’s no need for a capital letter.
Whole words or phrases
NEVER CAPITALISE WHOLE WORDS LIKE THIS. It’s hard to read, no matter how big the font or short the word. And it’s terrible for accessibility. Screen readers announce each letter individually and some people with dyslexia struggle to differentiate the letters when they’re all at the same height.
All words in a headline
When you use capitals for everything, except words like ‘a’, ‘of’ and ‘the’, it’s called title case. It can be hard to read and sounds overly formal and old fashioned. Not our tone at all. 'This Is an Example of Title Case'…so we use sentence case for headlines.
But what about consistency?
It might feel like using 'Online Banking' in one sentence and 'online banking' in another is inconsistent. But, done correctly, capitalisation plays an important role.
It makes things clear: people will know when we’re talking about a specific product, person or place. And when we’re talking more generally.
It helps people skim: proper nouns will stand out, so people can find the relevant information to them. Too many capitals will slow them down.
It keeps us on tone: the HSBC tone is to be human above all else. Peppering our writing with capital letters makes it feel formal, self-important and less approachable.
It’s standard practice when writing in English: everyone from newspapers to governments and household brands do it this way. So readers are used to it.
So, it’s okay to say:
There are lots of HSBC credit cards (common noun) to choose from, including our Rewards Credit Card (proper noun).
The Government (proper noun) has announced new banking reforms that build on the work of previous governments (common noun).
One exception: meta titles (also called title tags or browser titles)
These are short page descriptions that you see at the top of your browser tab or in your search results. SEO best practice is to use start case. That means capitalising every word, even thigs like ‘of’. And we use pipes (|), not standard punctuation, to separate sections or keywords.
For example:
Current Accounts | Everyday Banking – HSBC UK

Collective nouns
We use singular verbs for businesses and other organisations, such as HSBC, the Hong Kong Monetary Authority and the Reserve Bank of Australia. For example, the Financial Conduct Authority is a body which regulates the conduct of financial services firms in the UK.
There are a couple of exceptions: sports teams and the police normally take the plural. For example, Manchester United have broken their transfer record; police in Chennai are carrying out a major fraud investigation. However, this may not be the case in some markets, depending on local usage.

Contextual help
Contextual help is additional information we provide to help users complete their tasks - for example, using mobile or online banking. The basic instructions to users should always be immediately visible; contextual help may explain a term or provide useful but not crucial information. It can take on various forms, such as notifications, tooltips or expand links.
To achieve excellent contextual help copy:
Only include additional information that’s relevant to users completing their tasks
Make sure that users are directed to a specific area of the page, but take care to avoid constructs such as ‘Click the button on the right’. This won’t work well responsively or for users with screen readers

Currencies
Currency codes and currency symbols should be placed before the amount with no space. For example, £500 or GBP500 (A space could result in the amount being dropped into a different line).
UX design patterns relating to currencies, for example, in transaction lists or payment components, will be documented in Design Toolkits used by your business area. Seek guidance from your UX design colleagues on how currencies are formatted in components.
In scenarios where multiple currencies are shown, currency codes should typically be used to avoid the risk of confusion between currencies sharing the same symbol (eg $).
Use a comma to divide numbers higher than 999, with one comma preceding each group of three digits, such as: 1,000.00, 10,000.00 or 50,000,000. Alternative local market formatting patterns can be used to meet local customer expectations (in India, for example, use the Indian numbering system).
For large amounts, you can also suffix the number million, billion or trillion, or m, bn or tn if space is tight. For example, £50 billion or £50 bn. When writing for India, you can use 'lakh' and 'crore'. A 'lakh' equals 1,00,000, '10 lakhs' equals 10,00,000, a 'crore' equals 1,00,00,000 and '10 crores' equals 10,00,00,000.
Use sentence case when writing terms such as pound, dollar, euro, sterling, yen.
We use a minus symbol to represent something being deducted from the account, eg '-£10.00'. The minus symbol goes before the entire unit. The minus symbol is formatted in the same way to represent a negative amount, eg overdraft amount.

Date and time
Dates
In all markets except the US and the Philippines, when writing dates in full, use DD Month YYYY, without ordinals. For example: 1 October 2016 (don't use 1st October 2016 or October 1 2016). In the US and the Philippines, use Month DD, YYYY (for example: August 10, 2025 or August 10).
If space is restricted, you can shorten to, for example, 1 Oct 2016. The 3-letter month abbreviations are: Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun, Jul, Aug, Sep, Oct, Nov, and Dec.
When writing a range of dates, include all digits, even if within the same century. This is because, for digital copy, readers need to be able to scan information quickly and easily. For example:
1799 to 1837
1985 to 1986
Days of the week
Write days of the week in full where possible. For example, Monday. If space is restricted you can shorten to: Mon, Tue, Wed, Thu, Fri, Sat and Sun.
Don't use all caps (MON) and don't include a full stop/period (MON.).
Time
Time should be written in the 24-hour clock.
This is to avoid any confusion as the reader scans digital copy quickly. For example:
19:56
20:00 to 06:30
When you want to include a time zone, use an initialism, such as GMT: The call will begin at 4:00GMT.
There's flexibility to use the 12-hour clock where this is more prevalent in a market (such as in the UAE). If you do use the 12-hour clock, write am and pm in lower case, not AM and PM.
For example:
10:25am (separate minutes with a colon, not a full stop)
From 10 to 11am (use “to” where possible rather than a hyphen or dash)
Digital Accessibility guidance is that this pattern (using colons and "to") works best for the main screen readers including JAWS and NVDA.
Referring to 'midnight' (instead of 00:00) and midday (instead of 12pm) can be easier for the reader to understand. Also, midnight is the first minute of the day, not the last. Consider using “11:59pm” or "23:59" to avoid confusion.

Error messages and notifications
Guidance in this section is relevant to a range of feedback handing scenarios, such as error, information, warning and success messages / notifications.
There are a number of ways to help customers when they encounter these types of messages.
Clear solution
Make sure that it’s clear what customers need to do next. Describe the problem as usefully as possible and focus on how the issue should be resolved.
Error codes
If you need to include an error code, make sure you include them in a sentence, instead of as a title or in brackets, for example,
‘For more help, phone 020 2626 6252 and quote the error code GH-444.’
Tone of voice
Don’t forget to continue using our tone of voice – especially 'being human'! Use contractions.
Passive voice
Try to take ownership of an issue if it's our fault. For example, 'We're currently trying to fix the issue' instead of 'The issue is being investigated'.
You can use a passive voice if you want to shift the blame away from the customer. However, if you need a customer to resolve an error they’ve caused, make sure you’re clear.
Use ‘please’ when it seems appropriate
You can use the word ‘please’ at the beginning of an error/feedback message to nudge the user in the right way. However, this is optional.
Don’t over-apologise
Our rule of thumb is to apologise only when an error is HSBC’s fault or we haven’t explained something adequately.
Examples include errors where our server is down, or if we’ve not been clear enough about what information we want customers to provide.
However, it’s excessive to apologise for things like incorrect password entry, or errors where a customer isn’t authorised to see the content. It adds to our word count and testing has shown customers don’t expect an apology in most instances.
Sometimes we do have to point out why a user’s actions may have caused an error, like a '403' error where they’re not authorised to see content. In this instance we’d say:
We can’t show you this content. You may not be authorised to see it. For more information contact help@helpdesk.com.
Success messages
Join the customer in recognising and celebrating a moment of success, such as opening a new account, by using positive language. It may also be helpful to explain ‘what happens next’.
Warnings
Warning message copy should clearly communicate what the issue is and how it will affect the user:
Consider tone of voice, the more serious the issue, the more urgent your tone might become – but the language you use should be human and conversational
Present how the issue will affect the user, rather than what’s happening - for example, ‘Our service will be unavailable between…’ should be 'You won’t be able to access your accounts between…’.
Avoid using ‘Warning’ as a heading for these types of messages, instead, use a heading that will explain the issue

FAQs
FAQs are answers to common questions asked by customers. A good set of FAQs can help customers, and can cut down on the number of calls and complaints. However, consider whether FAQs are really needed. Could copy or design improvements remove the need for FAQs?
FAQs should normally be written out in full, as ‘Frequently asked questions’. ‘FAQs’ can appear only where space is limited.
Use plain, everyday language in response, write in first person, focus on one topic per question and be concise.
Example usage:
A title: Frequently asked questions
Directing users: Go to ‘Frequently asked questions’ to find out more.
Also, refer to the 'Punctuation' section of this style guide.

Footnotes
Footnotes should be used to reference additional information from a point within the main body of copy. Footnotes can supply short instructions for a form (*indicates required field) through to lengthier legal disclaimers.
Care should be taken when using footnotes. Ideally, all important information should be presented to the user upfront within the main body of copy.
Footnotes can be marked with an asterisk, numbers or symbols. Check with your local area and product team to understand the best marker to use, as well as the best placement of the footnote (at the bottom of the page, immediately beneath that section of copy or, in the case of instructions for forms, at the top-right of the form itself). Where an asterisk is used to indicate questions in a form that are either mandatory or non-mandatory, it should sit alongside the relevant questions.
Please do not mix symbols and numbers.
For footnoted copy that is likely to be referred back to in future (for example, terms and conditions or legal disclaimers), numbers are recommended so the user can more easily cite the relevant footnote in a query.

Fractions
In digital copy, we use the number format for fractions, for example, 1⁄3, 3⁄4, in tight spaces.
Where space allows, spell out the fraction (one third, three quarters). Screen readers can struggle to pick up a numerical representation of a fraction adequately – it might instead read out ‘three, forward slash, four’. This can be resolved if those fractions are represented as symbols, although this may in turn make them too small for other online users.

Gender
Avoid gendered terms like chairman (where ‘chair’ would do). If you have to use a gendered adjective, use ‘male’ and ‘female’, not ‘man’ and ‘woman’.
Never use ‘he’ as a generic pronoun. Use ‘they’ and ‘them’ instead.
For further guidance see the Inclusive Language guide.
Before
If you have any questions, contact your Relationship Manager and he'll be able to help you.
After
If you have any questions, contact your Relationship Manager and they’ll be able to help you.

Help text
Help text is a form of contextual help – it’s any copy that provides more information for the user. It can be included in-line, providing necessary information to help users complete forms quickly and accurately.
If you believe a user journey might be unclear, work with the designer on developing an area for help text, or other contextual help.
Ensure that the copy you provide:
Summarises the point as succinctly as possible – aim for one sentence
Doesn’t contain hyperlinks that would take users away from completing the form

Hint text (placeholder text)
The default state for hint text (also called placeholder or ghost text), which is the text within form fields, is for the field to be empty - no hint text.
However, use hint text where the value of including it is clear, such as to describe a data entry format such as '0.00' for an amount. And keep copy short.
Do say: '0.00' / 'dd/mm/yyyy' / 'eg High Street' / example@email.com /
Don't say: 'test@email.com' / 'Sarah' (or other names) as example name / 'Please type in...' / 'Blank' / 'Text field' / repeat a field label name.
Don't include important information in hint text - put this above the field or component. This is because hint text disappears once selected. Also, users with visual impairments may find it hard to read due to colour contrast. Other potentially impacted user groups could include cognitive-impaired / those with memory issues.
Remember the user will be shown an error message with further guidance if needed, which reduces the requirement for the hint text to cater to edge case issues.

HSBC
You can use either ‘HSBC is’ or ‘we are’. HSBC is a singular noun, so it’s not ‘HSBC are…’
It may be preferable to use ‘we are’ and ‘our’ where the reader can be expected to know what HSBC is - and it's more aligned to our tone of voice. Over-use of ‘HSBC’ in longer copy can also feel needlessly self-promotional.
Consider context carefully when deciding whether to use 'HSBC' as a prefix for products, propositions or features (eg 'HSBC' Flexible Saver Account).
Global Brand and Global Brand Legal have confirmed that from a brand design, legal and trademark perspective, there is no mandatory requirement to include an HSBC prefix when writing proposition and product names in any scenario.
Over-use of 'HSBC' may also impact usability and accessibility. For example, the use of an 'HSBC' prefix in a list of products or features in our apps may make navigating app content harder for screen reader users and customers with conditions making reading on a screen more challenging.
The addition of an HSBC prefix is more appropriate where the context means it may be unclear whether we're describing an HSBC-owned product, proposition or feature. It can also add impact when we want to give a 'hero' brand moment to a product, proposition or feature in our copy, but this is typcally less common.
So consider what's most appropriate for your scenario.
Please note: The ‘H’ in HSBC sounds as if it starts with a vowel (aitch) so we use ‘an’. It’s ‘an HSBC credit card’, not ‘a HSBC credit card’.

Hyphens
With hyphens, the main guideline to follow is this: if there's a risk of confusion or ambiguity without a hyphen, use one.
For this reason, use hyphens in compound adjectives that come before a noun:
Long-term investments
Our first-quarter results
Make sure you have an up-to-date ID card
However, if the same phrase comes after the noun, in most cases you shouldn't use a hyphen:
Investments for the long term
Our results from the first quarter
Make sure your ID card is up to date
And never use hyphens with adverbs (words ending in 'ly'), even before a noun. Write 'carefully selected funds' and not 'carefully-selected funds'.
When it comes to describing a range of time or values, don't use hyphens or dashes. Instead, use the word 'to':
Our lines are open Monday to Friday, 08:00 to 20:00
Earn bonus interest on deposits of £4,000 to £10,000
Writing it this way is better for users of screen readers, as a hyphen may not be read out.
For all other instances, if you're unsure whether a word should include a hyphen and it isn't included in our preferred terms, refer to our preferred dictionary and use the standard term.

Inclusive language
See our Inclusive Language guide.
We connect people, ideas and capital with our borderless approach to banking. And we carry that through into how we write, by being inclusive in our language.
No matter who our readers are, or where they’re based, we use words in a way that makes them feel seen and respected. And we never forget they’re real people with diverse beliefs, feelings and identities – so we always talk to them as such.

Links
Text links allow users to navigate between pages or content items. They’re used primarily for navigation but can also be used for low-priority calls to action or if there are multiple actions that are all the same priority.
Buttons are used for high-priority actions.
Make sure links work as effective signposts:
Describe the action the user is performing, where they’re navigating to (for example, ‘Go to online banking’) or the title of the document
Keep the link concise (don’t exceed 8 words)
Avoid meaningless copy (‘Click here’) and punctuation at the end
Always provide document type for download links
Best practice is to provide file size of document

Mobile, online, and telephone banking

Online banking

Modals
These pop-up boxes can be a form of notification, information or contextual help. They can also be used for taking customers through new products and user journeys, without taking them out of the journey they’re on.
Make sure that the modal title is clear and descriptive. Avoid general ‘Are you sure?’ copy that doesn’t immediately tell the customer what they need to do. Ideally, make the title work together with the CTA options. Don’t rely on ‘submit’ and ‘cancel’ if better direction and signposting can be given.
The modal title shouldn’t be an exact repeat of the copy within the modal. The best modals give users the information they need in the heading so that they can confidently select the action they need. Any additional (body) copy should offer succinct clarification and elaboration.
Although modals allow for more space, copy still needs to remain concise. However, this is an opportunity to increase TOV and helpfulness to our customers.

Neurodiversity
All people fall somewhere on the spectrum for neurodiversity, but those of us who fall outside a ‘normal range’ can experience challenges (at least one in ten working age adults). This includes people with autism spectrum conditions, dyslexia and dyscalculia.
There’s lots we can do as writers to help customers and colleagues with neurodiversity conditions. Read the HSBC neurodiversity guidelines to find out more.

Numbers
In digital copy, we write numbers as digits - 1,2,3 (not one, two, three). This is because digital copy usually needs to be direct and concise, delivering the information to the user as quickly as possible. Users often scan websites, picking out what they need, so numbers written as digits can stand out easily.
Numbers as digits are also useful for tight spaces – for example, on apps.
However, common sense should be applied for conversational text, in which cases 1-9 might be best written out (such as in this Guide).
Big numbers
Use a comma to divide numbers higher than 999, with one comma preceding each group of three digits, such as: 1,000.00, 10,000.00 or 50,000,000.
Localise this style when writing copy that is specific to a region. For example, for India, use the Indian numbering system. Starting from the right, the first comma should be placed after 3 digits, then every 2 digits (eg 1,00,000).
For large amounts, you can also suffix the number million, billion or trillion or m, bn or tn if space is limited. For example, £50 billion or £50 bn. When writing for the Indian market, you can use 'lakh' and 'crore'. A 'lakh' equals 100,000, '10 lakhs' equals 10,00,000, a 'crore' equals 1,00,00,000 and '10 crores' equals 10,00,00,000.
A solid block of numbers is difficult to read.
For UK and global copy, the format for telephone numbers is:
+ country code_(optional area number prefix)_area code_phone number
For example:
The number for HSBC Group head office in London is +44 (0)20 7991 8888
For local copy, follow the regional format. For example, in the US it’s very common to hyphenate each set of numbers, for example:
Please dial 1-999-8272
Always be consistent within your market.
If the copy is going to be available on a phone or tablet, it may be necessary to include phone numbers with no spaces in order for a customer to tap on it and automatically dial. It may also be useful to position the phone number at the end of a sentence to make it easier for users to click on it.

Progress trackers
Use the present tense if describing something currently ongoing or being done by the bank (for example, ‘Processing’).
Use the past tense for things that have already happened (for example, ‘Sent’) or steps that a user has already completed. Also use the past tense of a verb rather than adding ‘complete’ to the end of terms (for example, ‘Actioned’ and ‘Processed’ instead of ‘Action complete’ and ‘Processing complete’).
If the user doesn’t need to take an action or see an ongoing process, use more descriptive terms, such as ‘Payment details’ and ‘Confirmation’.
Use verbs (for example, ‘Review’ or ‘Confirm’) for those steps where a user will be able to input or edit information on the screen.

Punctuation and full stops
Although punctuation is crucial to understanding content clearly and quickly, too much punctuation can clutter the page and disrupt the reading experience. For this reason, we only use punctuation when absolutely necessary.
. (the full stop)
We don’t use full stops for:
Headings
Field labels
Button names
Lists
Any other navigational constructions unless the copy has more than one sentence
Don’t use a full stop after a title, for example, Mr Thomas and Ms Aleem.
!
Like all punctuation, the exclamation mark / point has its uses. However, as it usually designates surprise, humour or shock, it is rare that this will be appropriate for HSBC content. Please use with care.
%
For digital copy, it’s important that the reader can easily scan the text and understand the meaning within seconds. For this reason, we use the symbol ‘%’, and not ‘per cent’ or ‘percent’.
There’s no space between the number and the sign – for example, we would write ‘23%’.
( )
Avoid using brackets to create plural versions of a term, for example, ‘Select item(s)’. Just use the plural version, which of course includes choosing one or more.
/
Avoid using slashes if possible. You should usually be able to use ‘and’ or ‘or’ instead. But if you feel you have to use one, leave a space on either side, as this is better for accessibility.
Example: countries / regions
We don’t usually capitalise the words after the slash, even if the slash follows the first word of a sentence or is used as a table label.
Example: 'Countries / regions will benefit from the changes we're making'.
We would only capitalise the words after a slash if it’s a proper noun or HSBC product name.
Example: ‘Advance Bank Account / Student Account’ or ‘Liverpool / London offices’
‘ ’ and " ” (quotation marks)
Double quotation marks
Use double quotation marks when you’re quoting someone word for word:
“Our new learning hubs are not just about taking classes,” said a spokesperson.
Single quotation marks
Use single quotation marks to emphasise a particular phrase:
Visit an ‘island paradise’.
You can also use single quotation marks for clarity when directing users to buttons or other navigational items. For example:
Select ‘Cancel’ to exit.
Go to ‘Offers’ to find out more.
Tap 'Wallet & Apple Pay' and enter your card's security code.
This shouldn’t be an issue for screen readers. The accessibility team has advised that for most screen reader users, if text has ‘single quotes’ the words may just be read in a slightly higher pitch.
Single quotation marks should also be used to indicate quotations within quotations:
“Economists are now describing China’s Belt and Road Initiative as a ‘megatrend’ that will drive growth in countries far beyond Asia,” said a spokesperson.
Using punctuation
Punctuation marks, such as full stops and commas, are contained inside quotation marks if the quote completes the statement:
The HSBC spokesperson said: “I’m delighted the new branch is now open.”
The exception is when you're using a quotation as part of a longer sentence, and the quotation is not a sentence in its own right:
The economist John Maynard Keynes once said that words should be “a little wild”, though he was himself rather shy.
&
We recommend using ‘and’ wherever possible instead of the ampersand (&). The ampersand should only be used in calls to action and table headings, or where space is tight.
Some commonly used terms, such as ‘Terms & Conditions’, may have an ampersand, which is in line with general use. Please consult the Preferred Terms for clarity.

Responsive design
Responsive design means that content can be replicated on any size of device. Design components shrink, expand and reflow depending on whether the user is on a desktop, tablet or mobile. However, the copy itself will stay the same.
Wherever possible we write copy that can be used on all digital platforms, across desktop, mobile, tablet and any other relevant contexts.
Some tips include:
Avoiding platform-specific language such as ‘click’ (‘select’ works for touchscreens too)
Not making assumptions about how your content is displayed: using ‘above’ or ‘below’ in a form instruction may not work on a mobile
Keeping your copy short as it needs to fit smaller mobile screens - remember that long blocks of copy will be even harder for a customer to understand on mobile where it may take up several screens

Sort options
Sort options allow users to change the order of content on a page.
The sort option should be labelled with ‘Sort by: [followed by sort option], such as ‘Sort by: most popular’.
Each sort option should be labelled in a concise way, describing the effect of selecting it.

Superscript
A superscript is a number or asterisk that appears next to (and slightly above) text. It directs the user to supporting information, usually a footnote, elsewhere on the page. For example: Access a range of health benefits for you and your family2.
If a superscript relates to a particular word, term or number, it should be placed next to it. If it relates to the whole sentence it goes at the end of the sentence. For all cases, the superscript should be placed before any punctuation. For example:
Borrow between £1,000 and £25,000 with fixed monthly payments4.
Get a Personal Instalment Loan now to enjoy an APR as low as 2.38%3, with up to HK$8,300 cash rebate.
If there are 2 or more superscripts in a sentence, place them next to their respective related words. For example:
Get priority services, no HSBC fees on everyday transactions1 and $50 monthly maintenance fees2.
If there are 2 superscripts related to the same word, separate the superscripts with a comma. For example:
Give you and your family peace of mind with HSBC Premier Worldwide Travel Insurance1,2.
The most common forms of a superscript are either a number or an asterisk (*). You should only use an asterisk if there is no more than one footnote on the page. Don’t use a double asterisk (**).

'That' and 'which'
Simply put, use ‘which’ when the clause that follows isn’t essential to the meaning of the sentence – it just offers extra detail. Use ‘that’ when the clause is essential and affects the meaning.
Consider the different meaning of these two sentences:
If you want to close the bank account, which has been open since 2015, you need to visit your local branch
If you want to close the bank account that has been open since 2015, you need to visit your local branch
The first is giving extra detail about the account; the second is making specific reference to an account open since 2015.

'Your' v 'my' and 'we' v 'you'
Personal pronouns make our copy more human and easier to read.
Especially for app content, use 'your' rather than 'my', for consistency. For example, write ‘Manage your account’ not ‘Manage my account'.
Watch out for text that includes more ‘we’ than ‘you’ – that means we’re talking about ourselves, not the customer. Avoid clichéd terms like ‘At HSBC, we understand…’
Similarly, don’t put words in the customer’s mouth, as in ‘You’ll be delighted to know…’ It’s presumptuous and can irritate the customer.
