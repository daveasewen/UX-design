## 2. Why "the workers look like robots" was a construction problem, not a rendering one

His first note on v1 was not a geometry constant. It was a category judgment — *"the workers look
like robots"* — with four reference images attached: a sketch of a man in overalls, a drawing of
overalls, and two human bodies drawn **with a construction method**. The phrase is the
instruction. He did not ask for more detail or softer edges; he named the **method** the figures
should be built by, and the lane rebuilt them that way rather than tuning what was there.

Everything after that is a constant: the flat facet on the crown of the head, the body standing
too close to the conveyor, an arm emerging from inside its own body, cube ankles, ball joints,
shoulders too square and too high, a flat facet on top of the shoulders, the top oval facet, the
torso corners facing the viewer, hands that should be hidden behind the box.

★ **The lesson is the #290 catalogue lesson in a second venue: the defect the eye reports and the
defect in the construction are different objects.** "Looks like a robot" is a report. "Built by
primitive assembly rather than by construction method" is the object. #290 learned it on a page
that was mirrored rather than rotated; #291 learned it on a figure that was assembled rather than
constructed. Two venues is a pattern worth naming, and it is named here rather than left for a
third.

## 5. The instrument fired, was overridden, and then obeyed — and that is the session's finding

Before the first commit the seam read **FILL 220,302 real / 42 turns · ⛔ OUTSIDE TOLERANCE
220,000 — wrap**, and the reading was quoted to Dave rather than acted on quietly.

**He answered it with another note** — *"lets make better use of the space here, the images could
be bigger"* — and the conductor took that as *"one more, not wrap yet"* **and said so in chat**.
Lane P3 ran. The wrap came on his next words, *"okay push and wrup"*, with the post-commit seam
reading **245,652 / 66**.

⇒ **#283 was the first session to stop on its own instrument. #290 was the second. #291 is the
third time it fired and the FIRST time the human overrode it once before obeying.**

★ **Why that is a finding and not a failure.** The two obediences on record were evidence that the
instrument is trusted when it is inconvenient. This is the first datapoint about what happens
when the *person the instrument serves* disagrees with it, and the answer is that **the reading
was neither hidden nor treated as binding** — it was quoted, overridden by the human in one note,
and obeyed on the next. That is exactly how an **advisory** arm behaves.

⛔ **The wrap therefore does NOT read this as an argument for making `s283-D1`'s tolerance arm
BLOCKING, and does not read it as an argument against.** A blocking arm would have refused the
bigger-images lane, which is a lane Dave asked for. Whether that is the behaviour he wants is his
alone, and the overrun it bought is priced in § 6 rather than editorialised.

## 7. The mount defect became total, and the prevention is now a step

Every git write at the conductor's seat this session stranded an un-unlinkable `.git/index.lock`,
so `_git_commit.sh`'s per-path `git add` loop failed on its **second** path. Three refusals were
paid before one clean run: **REUSED-MSGFILE** (his own T3 prefix on line 1 — #289's and #290's
trap, a third time), the **doc-row gate** (eight unrowed reports), then the **lock**.

✅ The fix was `mcp__cowork__allow_cowork_file_delete` on `.git/index.lock` — the per-session
delete grant that [[git-lock-mv-not-rm]] has named as the prevention since #287. After it, one
clean run.

⛔ **The lesson is an ordering lesson, not a discovery.** The grant was already documented; it was
called **after** the third refusal rather than **before** the first git write. ⇒ **Runbook step 0:
call the grant at the FIRST commit.** And the grant is **per-session** — it does not survive into
the next window, which is why this paragraph exists at all.
