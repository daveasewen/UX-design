How to read it

A dot goes dark for four reasons, and only two of them are anyone’s fault.

Either the page has no way to draw the relation, or the relation was never written, or it was written and filed where the page hides it, or the dot is meant to stand alone. Each block below says which, from the data, and prices it in the only two currencies this record has: a lane, or one question to you.

The three settings are the page as it opens, the page with every family chip switched on, and that plus the Constitution.

The pink cloud — 100 of 145 UX principles

This is the picture on the screenshot. The Explanation view holds 145 principles and 30 polarities. Every polarity is wired. The principles are not: a principle gets a line only when a polarity names it as a party, and between them the 30 polarities reach 45 principles. The other 100 have no edge of any kind in storage — not a null, not a stub, nothing.

The wiring is already written. The principle generator can emit two more edge kinds and both are held back by the ruling that landed the family: one groups each principle under the research family it came from (145 of the principles carry that field, across 32 families), the other links a principle to the sources it was graded on (145 carry it). Turning the first on gives every one of the 100 dark dots a line on the day it runs.

It also puts a family node into the graph, which is a new kind of thing on the stage — and that is the part only you can say.

What wires it
The principle generator’s two held-back edge kinds, landed behind the same ruling that landed the family.
What it costs
One question to you, then one lane. The generator is written; the lane lands the edges, re-runs the validator and proves the count moved.
