## 0. The answer, first

A matrix earns its place when a brief leaves two open decisions that each change the picture, and it is noise otherwise. The instrument that fits Apollo is not Zwicky's full box (every parameter, every value, every cell) but the pruned, rendered, two-axis cut of it: the axes are the decisions the agent would otherwise make silently, the cells are rendered pages, one cell carries the recommendation with a reason quoted from the brief, and one cell is a deliberate foil. Sixty years of the literature say four things that bear on the build:

1. The box blows up; every serious user prunes it before looking (Ritchey's cross-consistency assessment, Heller et al.'s "dilemma"). Two axes, three at most as slices. Six cells is the working size; nine is the ceiling.
2. Several alternatives side by side beat one at a time — for the quality of the result (Dow et al. 2010), for the honesty of the feedback (Tohidi et al. 2006), and for keeping options open until the evidence is in (Sobek, Ward and Liker 1999).
3. The box's known failure is post-hoc justification — a designer picks intuitively, then fills in the box to argue for it (Tomiyama, cited in Heller et al. 2014). The countermeasure is procedural: render the cells before the recommendation is written, cite the brief's sentence for it, and always carry a foil.
4. Picks become taste only by accumulation. The rigorous route is a preference model over pairwise picks (Bradley–Terry 1952 → conjoint 1978 → Christiano et al. 2017); the honest first step for Apollo is counting — the same value winning on the same axis across matrices — and a candidate ruling put to Dave in prose. Nothing is learned into the law without his word.

The worked example (§7) runs the CEO international-banking brief through the axis finder: seven decisions the agent would make silently, two of which the brief itself closes, two of which dominate the picture. The matrix is 3 × 2 (five live cells after one is struck as incoherent), with exposure-chart form as a slice on the picked cell. The #291 record (§7.4) shows the instrument's honest limit: a matrix would have saved the first two of six rounds (the choice of kind) and none of the last four (the tuning of the chosen one).

## 1. The lineage — what each method fixes

**Zwicky's morphological box (1940s; Science, 21 March 1969; the Macmillan book of the same year).** Name the parameters of a problem, list every value each can take, and the space of solutions is the product. Zwicky's claim was completeness — no solution left unconsidered because nobody thought of it. He applied it to jet engines and astronomy. The box is the ancestor of the receipt's "matrix of options": axes and cells. What it does not have is a picture in the cell or a recommendation; Zwicky's box is a census, not a proposal.

**Ritchey's General Morphological Analysis, with cross-consistency assessment (Swedish Defence Research Agency from 1995; swemorph.com; Ritchey 2011, Springer).** The fix for the blow-up. Values are compared pairwise, not cell by cell, and any pair that cannot coexist strikes every cell containing it. Ritchey: "a morphological field involving as many as 100,000 formal configurations requires no more than a few hundred pair-wise evaluations in order to create a solution space." Growth in the pairwise work is quadratic in the number of values, not exponential in the number of parameters — "naturally, there are practical limits reached even with quadratic growth." For Apollo: CCA is the step between the axis finder and the renderer. It runs on value pairs, so a 3 × 2 matrix costs six checks, and the struck cell is shown struck (a greyed cell with the reason), not silently omitted — the struck cell is evidence that the axes were real.

**The morphological chart in engineering design (Pahl and Beitz; Ulrich and Eppinger's "concept combination table").** Rows are sub-functions, cells are candidate solutions for each, and a concept is a path through the chart, one cell per row. This is the form that survives in every engineering-design textbook, and its known pathology is the one Heller, Schmid, Löwer and Feldhusen name in "The dilemma of morphological analysis in product concept synthesis" (DESIGN 2014, Dubrovnik, 19–22 May 2014): "approaches relying on combinations of single elements axiomatically suffer from combinatorial explosion" — seven sub-functions with seven solutions each is 823,543 overall solutions and 1,029 compatibility comparisons. Their review of the remedies (Birkhofer's optimal box dimensions, hierarchical decomposition, Pareto filtering, sampling) all amount to the same instruction: cut the box before you look at it. And the line that matters most for the honesty of a recommendation, citing Tomiyama et al.: "operators could use the box as an argumentation for their personally and in most cases intuitively found solutions as opposed to the intended procedure for the morphological analysis." A matrix drawn after the pick is a rationalisation. The order of operations is the guard.

**Pugh's concept selection / controlled convergence (1980s; Total Design, Addison-Wesley 1991).** Not a generator but a chooser: pick a datum concept, score every other concept against criteria as better (+), worse (−) or same (S) than the datum, then iterate — combine the strong ones into hybrids, raise the detail of the criteria, re-run with a stronger datum. The Cambridge IfM page describes it as "a non-numeric and iterative tool for concept selection, which has joint goals of both improving and narrowing the choice of concepts available." Two things transfer. The datum: Assembly's default composition for a brief is the natural datum cell, and every other cell is scored against it, which is exactly the "one deliberate foil" made systematic. The hybrid round: Pugh's second round is the receipt's "combine — this row's structure with that column's density," and it belongs after the first pick, not in the first grid.

**Set-based concurrent engineering (Sobek, Ward and Liker, "Toyota's principles of set-based concurrent engineering", MIT Sloan Management Review 40(2), Winter 1999, 31–40).** Toyota's practice of carrying several alternatives forward and narrowing gradually as evidence arrives, rather than picking one early and iterating on it. The three principles, as the paper is standardly summarised (the article itself is paywalled; the summary is from general knowledge, not fetched tonight): map the design space (define feasible regions, explore trade-offs by designing multiple alternatives, communicate sets of possibilities), integrate by intersection (look for intersections of feasible sets, impose minimum constraint), establish feasibility before commitment (narrow gradually while increasing detail, stay within sets once committed). For Apollo this is the argument that Studio's Explore should keep more than one cell alive after the first pick — the picked cell goes forward, the runner-up stays on the record — and that a gate refusing a cell is intersection working as intended.

**Orthogonal arrays / fractional factorial design (Plackett and Burman, Biometrika 33(4), 1946; Taguchi, Introduction to Quality Engineering, 1986).** When three axes are genuinely open, do not render 27 cells. An L9 array covers every pair of values across three three-valued axes in nine cells. This is the only respectable way to hold a third axis in a single grid; the alternative — a third axis as slices of the picked cell — is simpler to read and is the recommendation here (§4).

**TRIZ's contradiction matrix (Altshuller; Creativity as an Exact Science, English 1984).** Rows and columns are the parameters that improve and worsen, the cell is a list of inventive principles that have historically resolved that pair. Not a design-space matrix; a lookup table. Its relevance is narrow but real: Apollo's polarities (s238-D1, tensions become polarities) are contradiction pairs, and a rendered cell that sits on a polarity could carry the polarity's name. Not for the first build.

**Conjoint analysis (Luce and Tukey 1964; Green and Rao 1971; Green and Srinivasan, "Conjoint analysis in consumer research: issues and outlook", Journal of Consumer Research 5(2), 1978, 103–123).** Infer the weight a chooser puts on each attribute from their choices among bundles of attributes. This is the statistical form of "turn his picks into learned taste": each matrix pick is a choice among bundles (structure × emphasis), and enough of them recover the weights. The catch is sample size — conjoint needs dozens of choices per respondent to be stable, and Apollo will have one respondent making one pick per brief. §6 says what to do instead until the record is long enough.

**Pairwise preference learning (Bradley and Terry, Biometrika 39, 1952; Christiano, Leike, Brown, Martic, Legg and Amodei, "Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences", arXiv 1706.03741, June 2017, NeurIPS 2017).** The modern instance: a reward model fitted to pairwise human comparisons, with Christiano et al. reporting that comparisons on less than 1% of the agent's interactions were enough to shape behaviour. The form that carries over is not the model but the record: a pick is stored as "cell A preferred to cells B…F under brief X", which is the datum a model could later be fitted to. Store the comparison, not only the winner.

## 2. Rendered alternatives — the HCI record

**Design Galleries (Marks et al., SIGGRAPH 1997, Harvard/MERL).** The direct ancestor of "a matrix of rendered options." The abstract: "Design Gallery interfaces present the user with the broadest selection, automatically generated and organized, of perceptually different graphics or animations that can be produced by varying a given input-parameter vector. The principal technical challenges posed by the DG approach are dispersion, finding a set of input-parameter vectors that optimally disperses the resulting output-value vectors, and arrangement, organizing the resulting graphics for easy and intuitive browsing." Two lessons. Dispersion is measured in output space, not input space — two cells that look alike are one cell, whatever their settings say; for Apollo the axis finder should rank axes by how much the rendered page changes, not by how many settings they touch. Arrangement is the second problem, and a labelled two-axis grid is the arrangement that needs no explanation.

**Getting the right design and the design right (Tohidi, Buxton, Baecker and Sellen, CHI 2006).** "When presented with a single design, users give significantly higher ratings and less negative feedback, compared to when presented with the same design in a group of three alternatives." One rendered page invites acceptance; three invite the critical sentence. The receipt's guard — a pick with no sentence is a click — is the same finding from the other side: the matrix exists to draw the sentence out. Tohidi et al. also warn that testing "is a means to identify problems, not provide solutions"; the matrix should not expect his pick to redesign the cell, only to choose it and say why.

**Parallel prototyping (Dow, Glassco, Kass, Schwarz, Schwartz and Klemmer, ACM TOCHI, December 2010).** Same number of prototypes and the same feedback, but three at a time then two, against one at a time five times. Parallel won on the market (445.0 against 397.9 clicks per million impressions, χ² = 4.60, p < 0.05), on expert rating (24.4 against 21.7; the three top-rated ads all parallel), and on divergence (serial ads "deemed significantly more similar", F(1,14816) = 239.3). The explanation the paper offers is the one that matters for Studio: "parallel offers people distance between ego and object; serial conflates them" — and serial participants reported fixation: "I pretty much stuck with the same idea." Read against #291 (§7.4): six serial rounds on one figure is the serial condition.

**Scout (Swearngin, Wang, Oleson, Fogarty and Ko, CHI 2020).** "Prior constraint-based layout systems use low-level spatial constraints and generally produce a single design. To support designer exploration of alternatives, Scout introduces high-level constraints based on design concepts (e.g., semantic structure, emphasis, order) and formalizes them into low-level spatial constraints that a solver uses to generate potential layouts." With 18 designers it "helps designers create more spatially diverse layouts with similar quality" and "can help designers avoid a linear design process." Scout's axis vocabulary — structure, emphasis, order — is a tested starting list for Apollo's axis finder (§3), and its lock/prevent feedback on an alternative is the receipt's "lock and re-roll."

**Luminate (Suh, Chen, Min, Li and Xia, CHI '24, Honolulu, 11–16 May 2024; arXiv 2310.12953).** The LLM-era version of the axis finder. The system prompts the model to derive the dimensions of the design space from the user's prompt — "up to 5 nominal dimensions with up to 8 different labels and up to 3 ordinal dimensions with 5 levels" — then samples "up to 40 responses per prompt" by picking a value per dimension so that "responses will cover many sub-spaces within the overall design space," and lets users add dimensions of their own. Fourteen professional writers scored it 82.16 on the Creativity Support Index, highest on Exploration. Two cautions for Apollo. Forty text responses on a scatter plot is a writer's exploring instrument, not a designer's deciding instrument; Luminate is evidence that a model can name the axes from a brief, not that forty cells should be shown. And its dimensions are generated fresh each time; Apollo's should come from a fixed vocabulary first (§3) so that picks on the same axis are comparable across briefs — which is the whole basis of promotion.

## 3. How the axes are found from a brief

The axes are the decisions the agent would otherwise make silently. Three sources agree on how to surface them, and they compose into one procedure.

1. **Start from a fixed vocabulary of composition decisions, not a blank page.** Scout's tested list is structure, emphasis, order. Apollo's own record adds the ones its briefs have actually turned on: density (W1, #247), rhythm (W2, #248), the list/card line (#274, surface alone), chart form per question (the 27 chart receipts), drill mechanism (inline against sub-page), filter placement, and figure or illustration kind (#291). A vocabulary of eight to ten named axes, each with three or four named values, is the morphological field; a brief opens some of them and closes the rest.
2. **Ask of each axis, "does the brief settle this?"** This is the Jev-shaped question the receipt named. The CEO brief settles density in four words ("desktop first, comfortable density") and the axis finder must not reopen it — a matrix that re-asks a closed question is the "dot-to-dot book" in reverse. A settled axis is listed as settled, with the brief's sentence, on the matrix page; that listing is how Dave sees the agent read the brief.
3. **Rank the open axes by how much they change the picture** — Design Galleries' dispersion in output space. Cheapest proxy without a render: whether the axis changes the page's structure (what is where), its emphasis (what the eye lands on first), or only its finish. Structure and emphasis axes outrank finish axes; a finish axis (corner radius, a token value) is never a matrix axis, it is a tuner.
4. **Keep two. A third, if it survives the ranking, is shown as slices of the picked cell, not as a third dimension of the grid.** Then run cross-consistency on the value pairs and strike what cannot coexist, showing the strike.

The procedure is ordinary enough to be code: the vocabulary is a JSON file, step 2 is a per-axis question with the brief as context (Jev candidate, or a plain Opus call returning settled/open with the sentence), step 3 is a fixed priority order per axis class with a tie-break by the model, step 4 is a pair table. The rendered cells are the only expensive part.

## 4. When a matrix helps and when it is noise

**Cell count.** The choice-overload literature does not support a hard number. Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd's meta-analysis (Journal of Consumer Research 37(3), 2010, 409–425; 63 conditions, 50 experiments, N = 5,036) found "a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies." Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2015; 57 studies in 21 papers) found the effect is real under four moderators: choice-set complexity ("not so much about the absolute number of options but rather how complex choosing among them is"), decision-task difficulty (time pressure), preference uncertainty ("the more you know about your preferences, the easier it is to make a choice"), and decision goal (choosing against browsing). Read for Apollo: the chooser has low preference uncertainty (his eye is the instrument the whole record runs on), so the risk is not the count but the complexity of the set — cells that differ on several things at once. Labelled axes with everything else held constant across a row are the cure, and they are what separates a matrix from a sample. Midjourney's four-up grid (four images per prompt, U1–U4 to upscale, V1–V4 to vary, a re-roll; ASLA guide, 25 July 2024) and v0's three variants per prompt (DevClass, 10 October 2023) are samples: no axis is named, so a pick teaches nothing reusable. Stable Diffusion's X/Y/Z plot (AUTOMATIC1111 wiki: "creates multiple grids of images with varying parameters," rows and columns as X and Y, Z as a batch) and Figma's variant sets ("multiple dimensions of variation and a key:value pair naming convention, like state='hover' and style='secondary'", Figma blog, October 2020) are maps: the axis is named and the pick is a value on it. Apollo's matrix is a map. Working size six cells (3 × 2); nine (3 × 3) the ceiling; 81 never.

**When it helps.** Two or more open axes of the structure or emphasis class after the brief has been read (§3). A new page or a new kind of output. A brief that has bounced once — his refusal of a single proposal is the strongest signal that the silent decisions were wrong, and #280 is the case: the fifth sketch was refused and the matrix was ruled.

**When it is noise.** One open axis: that is #281's form, a recommendation on every set with one alternative, not a grid. A repair or a tuning pass on an accepted output: #291's rounds three to six (shoulders, facets, a figure stepping back) are edits to a chosen cell; the instrument there is lock-and-re-roll on one axis, or simply the edit. A brief that closes everything: a matrix of one live cell is a slow way to say yes. Axes that are not independent: if choosing a row fixes the column, it is a list of three, not a grid of nine, and drawing the grid hides that.

**The honesty of the recommendation.** Four procedural guards, each from a named failure: render before recommending (Tomiyama's post-hoc box); cite the brief's sentence for the recommended cell, and a cell with no sentence behind it cannot be the recommendation; always one foil, ideally the datum — what Assembly would have shipped unasked (Pugh's datum); and record the runner-up alongside the pick (set-based narrowing). A recommendation without a foil is a single design, and Tohidi et al. measured what a single design does to feedback.

## 5. The matrix spec, as the research shapes it

Refines the receipt's four-part spec; H3 draws it.

- brief: path and the sentences that closed axes.
- axes: two, each with name (from the vocabulary), values (three at most), and the reason it is open in plain words; a third axis marked "slice", applied to the picked cell only.
- cross-consistency: the value pairs struck, with the reason, shown on the page.
- cells: rendered at one breakpoint and one mode for the grid (the receipt's "thumbnails"), the recommended and the foil cells also at the second breakpoint; every cell shares everything not on an axis (Design Galleries' dispersion in output, nothing else varying).
- recommendation: one cell, its reason quoting the brief; the foil named as foil; the runner-up named.
- pick: the record described in §6, written only when he acts.

## 6. Picks, promotion, and the hand-off from Explore to Assembly

The receipt's split stands and the literature sharpens it: a pick is a choice among bundles under one brief (conjoint's datum); a ruling is law. They are different records with different writers.

**What the ruling store is.** `knowledge/_rulings.json` rows carry id, ruled, date, by ("Dave"), says (his words, verbatim, with where they were said), governs (paths), evidence, status — read at s311-D2 on 2026-09-30. Every field is his; the `says` field is his sentence with the export it came from. A pick has none of that shape: it is a value on an axis, under a brief, with or without a sentence. So picks do not go in `_rulings.json`, and the receipt's "unverified" is now verified: the store's own form refuses them.

**The pick record (proposed form, H3's to draw, his to rule).** One row per matrix, appended to a picks file beside the matrix pages (a JSONL under `notes/_matrices/` or a `knowledge/_picks.jsonl`; the location is a question for him, not this lane): matrix id · date · brief path · the two axes with all their values · the recommended cell · the picked cell (values on each axis) · the cells it was preferred to (the comparison, per §1's Bradley–Terry point) · his sentence verbatim, or `click` if none (the #272 separation, 79 clicks named as clicks) · any axis he rejected, with his words · the promotion state (none / candidate / promoted to s-id). Two things this form makes possible that a winner-only record does not: a preference model later, if the record grows; and the audit "did the recommendation win?" per matrix, which is the honesty measure of the axis finder and the recommender over time.

**Promotion.** When the same value wins on the same axis under three matrices with a sentence each (three is this lane's proposal; the number is his), the system writes a candidate ruling — "dashboards default to three bands", say — onto a review page with the three picks and sentences as its evidence, and a recommendation to promote, decline, or wait. He rules in prose; the existing `_inscribe_ruling.py` inscribes; the ruling's `evidence` field points at the picks. Only then does Assembly's composer read it as a default. A click-only run of three does not trigger; three clicks are three tastes with no reason, and a reasonless law is the thing the record exists to prevent. A rejected axis ("the question was wrong") goes to the vocabulary file as a note against that axis, never to the store.

**The hand-off, concretely.** Explore ends with a picked cell and its pick row. Promotion is one path from it: value → candidate ruling → his word → law → Assembly default. The other path is direct and needs no ruling: the picked cell is itself a page, and it goes to Assembly's Adapt or Check as an output with a pick behind it — governed from that point by the gates, which in Assembly block. So a pick changes one output at once and the law only by accumulation and his word; that is the whole safety of the split. What Assembly never does is read the picks file: it reads rulings. The picks file is Studio's, and the review page is the only door between them.

### 7.1 The axis finder's pass — every decision the agent would make silently

Seven decisions found; three closed by the brief's own sentences; two dominate the picture; one is a slice; two are left to Assembly's defaults and said so on the page.

### 7.2 Cross-consistency

Feed × counts-first is struck: a feed is item-led by its nature, so "counts first" in that row has nothing to be first of. Five live cells. The strike is shown on the page with that sentence.

### 7.3 The grid, the recommendation, the foil

Rows: three bands · tile wall · feed. Columns: counts first · items first. Everything else held constant: the dashboard set as it stands, comfortable density, light mode, 1440 for the grid.

Recommendation: three bands × items first. Reason, from the brief: the CEO wants three things "in this order" and must "answer the three questions without scrolling" — bands are the order made visible, and "each one leading to the record they can act on" is met by showing the records, not a number about them. Foil: tile wall × counts first — the shape a bare dashboard prompt defaults to and so the datum (whether the #246/#258 cold runs actually landed there is checkable in `reviews/COLDRUN-258-*.html`; not checked tonight); it answers "how many" and defers "which" to a click. Runner-up: three bands × counts first, if the approvals list proves too tall for the fold at 1440 — the one fact a render settles and prose cannot. Slice on the picked cell: exposure as bars by region against a map; the recommendation is bars, because the brief's drill is "positions and limits," which are rows, and a map with no geography in the data is decoration; the map is the slice's foil.

What this example demonstrates: the brief closed three axes and the page says so with the sentences; the two open axes were found from a vocabulary, not invented; the recommendation cites the brief twice; the foil is what Assembly would have done; a render decides the runner-up. None of it required Dave until the pick.

### 7.4 The #291 test — would a matrix have landed the workers in one or two?

His eleven entries (`notes/_lanes/291/DAVE-RULINGS-2026-09-20.md`). Round one: "the workers look like robots" with four reference images — that is an axis (figure kind: robot / construction-method figure / sketched figure) that the first lane chose silently and wrongly, and a 3 × 2 matrix of figure kind × pose would very likely have taken rounds one and two together. Rounds three to seven — facets removed, shoulders rounded and lowered, a body stepping back, hands hidden — are edits to the chosen cell, one constant at a time; no matrix helps there, and drawing one would have been the noise case. The honest claim for the proposal page: the matrix compresses the choice of kind, not the tuning of the chosen one. Six rounds become perhaps four, not two.

## 8. Recommendations to H3 (nothing here is ruled)

1. Draw the matrix as a map, not a sample: two named axes, three values at most each, cells sharing everything not on an axis; a struck cell shown struck.
2. Draw the axis finder as the page's first panel — closed axes with the brief's sentences, open axes ranked — so the matrix is visibly the reading of the brief.
3. The recommendation cites a sentence of the brief; a foil is always present and is the Assembly default where one exists; the runner-up is named.
4. The pick record is its own file with the §6 fields; `_rulings.json` is never written by a pick.
5. Promotion by accumulation with sentences, onto a review page, by his word, through `_inscribe_ruling.py`; the threshold is his to set (three proposed).
6. Build order as the receipt had it: static grid with pick and sentence → lock and re-roll on one axis → combine (Pugh's hybrid round) last.
7. The first live test is a real brief, not #291's drawing: the CEO overview at §7, rendered, is the candidate, because the answer is known enough to check the recommender against.
