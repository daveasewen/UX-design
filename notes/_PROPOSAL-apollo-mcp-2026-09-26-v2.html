Yes, we can have our own. But not our own protocol. Apollo should speak the two standards that already exist: publish our parts as an A2UI catalogue served through MCP, and where a host can't draw A2UI, send an MCP App running Apollo's own renderer. We own what neither standard has: knowing when each part is right, gating before anything is drawn, checking what each person may see, and recording every screen. The proof of concept is June's contextual dashboard, on mock data, for four mock banking roles.

Part one

The target architecture

Its shape only. Enough to choose a route and to know what the proof of concept must not paint out.

The field, in plain words

Three layers. The standards own the wire; Apollo brings the parts and the checks.

LayerWhat it meansWho fills it todayApollo's part
The wireHow messages travel between the agent, its tools and the app.MCP (agent to tools). AG-UI (agent to app, streamed). A2A (agent to agent).An MCP server. We use the wire; we don't make one.
The screen, as dataWhat to draw, written as a description rather than as code.A2UI. Microsoft Adaptive Cards. Vercel's json-render.The catalogue: our parts, their settings and their rules.
The drawingWho turns the description into pixels.A2UI renderers for React, Flutter, Lit, Angular and Android. MCP Apps: a sealed frame running the server's own HTML.Apollo's renderer, drawing canon markup. The same renderer runs inside the MCP Apps frame.

A2UI

Google · v0.9.1 in production · v1.0 due Q4 2026

The agent sends a description of the screen as data, never code. The app keeps a catalogue of approved parts and draws them itself.[1][3][4]

Google's own guidance: a catalogue should "directly reflect a client's design system", and be versioned like an API.[5]

For us: the catalogue is exactly where Apollo fits.

MCP Apps, and MCP-UI

Official MCP extension since 26 January 2026

A tool points to an HTML page, and the host shows it in a sealed frame inside the chat. The widest reach today: Claude, ChatGPT, Microsoft 365 Copilot, VS Code Copilot, Cursor and more.[14][41]

MCP-UI is the community project it grew from. It now says its packages "implement the spec, and serve as a community playground".[17]

For us: the fallback shell, carrying our renderer.

OpenAI Apps SDK

ChatGPT · built on MCP

ChatGPT's way of putting apps into the chat. ChatGPT now implements the MCP Apps standard; its own hooks stay on as compatibility aliases.[21][22]

For us: no separate work. MCP Apps covers it.

AG-UI

CopilotKit · an event stream

A live stream of events between an agent and an app: text, tool calls, state. In its makers' words, it "is not a generative UI specification"; it carries A2UI or MCP Apps.[23][24]

For us: a road we could add later, not a choice now.

Re-checked today, 26 September

  • A2UI: unchanged. Still v0.9.1 in production; v1.0 still a release candidate aimed at Q4 2026. The roadmap was last updated in June, and the iPhone and Mac renderer is still marked planned.[3][6]
  • MCP Apps hosts: firmer. The official client list now names ChatGPT, Cursor and PostHog Code. The first pass had those only from a secondary source.[41]
  • A2UI in chat hosts: narrower. No general chat host (Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, the Gemini app) draws A2UI itself. Gemini Enterprise does, but only for agents registered through its agent-to-agent route.[7] This is why the fallback shell matters.
  • MCP itself: new to this page. July's spec made MCP stateless and put tool names in headers, so a bank's gateway can route and authorise a call without opening it.[20]
  • Bank sign-in: new to this page. Enterprise-managed authorisation is now an official MCP extension. The bank's identity provider decides who can reach which server.[42]
  • Our fallback is Google's own pattern. Google's June post names "packaging the A2UI rendering engine directly within the MCP App bundle" as a supported way to reach hosts that don't draw A2UI.[12]

Three routes, side by side

Build our own, speak the standards, or ship pages.

A

Our own protocol

From scratch
What we ownEverything: the message format, the renderers, and the work of getting each host to support it.
ReachNone on day one. No host speaks it; each would need our renderer built in.
CostThe highest. A protocol, a renderer per platform, and keeping pace with two standards that move every quarter.

VerdictNo. It rebuilds what Google and the MCP group give away, and owns nothing extra that matters.

VerdictYes. We speak both standards and own only what is ours: the knowledge and the checks.

How one screen gets made

The agent asks. Apollo chooses, checks and sends. The host draws. The graph remembers.

Steps one to four run on the server, before anything reaches the person. The host never decides what is allowed; it only draws what Apollo sent.

What Apollo brings that the specs don't

The standards say what may be drawn. Apollo says what should be.

A2UI and MCP Apps both stop at "these parts are allowed". Neither says which part is right, whether it is accessible, who may see it, or what was shown. Those four are Apollo's.

  1. It knows when to use each part.

    Each part's notes say which question it answers, what shape of data it takes and which rulings govern it. The when-rules make the shortlist; a judgement model may rank inside it, never outside it.

    Today 39 of 138 parts carry a when-rule. The proof of concept's parts need them all.

  2. Gates run before anything is drawn.

    Neither spec requires accessibility: A2UI lists it as long-term vision, and MCP Apps is silent. Apollo runs its gates on the server. A screen that fails is not sent.

  3. What you may see is settled at the door, not in the screen.

    Entitlements are enforced where the data is fetched, at the MCP tool, never by hiding things in the interface. Anything that moves money is data-only: no HTML or script from the server.

  4. Every screen leaves a record.

    The knowledge graph becomes the audit trail: which catalogue version, which parts, which rulings each one obeyed, and which entitlement decisions were made, for every screen sent. Neither spec says anything about replay or audit.

Judgement at run time

The rules make the shortlist. Jev could rank it.

Jev was held back at build time to keep Apollo easy to hand on. It is not ruled out for a live GenUI layer. So the architecture carries two ways to choose, and you pick whether the second one is switched on.

The baseline · rules only

How it chooses
The when-rules and a fixed evaluator pick the parts and their order.
What it buys
Works anywhere, with no key and nothing to call out to. The same request gives the same screen.
What it costs
The order is only as good as the rules, and where they tie or are silent it falls to a fixed default.

The option · rules plus Jev

How it chooses
The rules make the shortlist. Jev ranks inside it, or reads the request for its intent and data shape. It can never add a part the rules did not allow.
What it buys
Judgement where the rules tie or say nothing, such as which of three eligible charts fits this question.
What it costs
A dependency, an API key and an outside service, which makes the GenUI layer less portable. A network hop: 579 to 621 ms per call when measured in September, three calls. One more thing that can be down.
How it degrades
Slow, down or no key: the server uses the rules' order, and the record says so.

Either way the record keeps Jev's answer beside the screen, so a screen replays from the record, not by asking again. Build time does not change: no gate or build step calls Jev.

Part two

The proof of concept

Defined in full. June's contextual dashboard, on mock data, for four mock banking roles. It is the first milestone, not the fifth.

Scope

One landing dashboard that assembles itself for whoever asks.

A Global Transaction Banking landing screen. The person asks a plain question such as "what needs my attention this morning?", and Apollo builds the answer from certified parts, for their role, at that time of day.

In

  • About 15 parts: headline figures, status, lists, a few charts, the page frame.
  • Mock company data: balances, payments awaiting approval, limits, exceptions.
  • Time triggers: cut-offs and month end.
  • Four mock roles, enforced at the tool call.
  • Two hosts: Claude Desktop through MCP Apps, and a small A2UI host of our own.
  • A record in the graph for every screen sent.
  • Jev as a switch, off by default.

Deliberately out

  • HSBC's real identity and entitlement services.
  • Any real customer or bank data.
  • Moving money. Screens can show and prepare, never execute.
  • Email and Teams signals, as June's note said.
  • The other 120-odd parts, and other transports such as A2A and AG-UI.
  • A security review and anything hosted beyond your machine.

What it proves

  1. A screen can be described as data and drawn to match its reference, by eye and by gate.

  2. A bad screen is refused before anyone sees it.

  3. The same request gives two different, correct screens for two roles, and the record says why.

  4. The same screen reaches a chat host and our own host from one server.

  5. Whether Jev's ranking is worth its cost, side by side with the rules alone.

How it is enforced

  1. Every tool call carries the mock role. The server checks the role against the tool and the data scope before any data is fetched.

  2. A part is only eligible if every tool it needs was allowed. Refused data never reaches the chooser, so it can't be drawn.

  3. Every grant and every refusal is written into the screen's record: role, tool, scope, allowed or refused.

The build path

Milestone one is the proof of concept. Everything else comes after it.

Estimates are in sessions, and they are estimates, not measurements. Steps one to four pay back at design time too.

Milestone one · the proof of concept

  1. The catalogue.

    Generate an A2UI catalogue from the metas for the dashboard's 15 or so parts, with when-rules for each.

    Proves · our notes are enough to describe a part as data

    Estimate: 1 to 2 sessions.

  2. The renderer.

    One renderer that turns an A2UI screen into canon markup, grown from the chart renderer. The same file runs inside the MCP Apps frame.

    Proves · a screen drawn from data matches its reference, by eye and by gate

    Estimate: 2 to 3 sessions.

  3. Gates as a service.

    The July idea, built: gates that take a screen in memory and return a verdict, served as MCP tools.

    Proves · a bad screen is refused before it is drawn

    Estimate: 2 sessions. The static checks take 0.68 s on a file today.

  4. The chooser.

    A fixed evaluator over the when-rules, with a seam where a ranker can plug in.

    Proves · same request, same screen; no candlestick in a cash view

    Estimate: 2 to 3 sessions.

  5. The dashboard, end to end.

    Mock data, the four mock roles at the tool, the record per screen, both hosts. The worked example above, running.

    Proves · one request, two roles, two correct screens, with the reasons on record

    Estimate: 3 to 4 sessions.

  6. Jev, side by side.

    Switch the ranker on and run the same requests both ways, so you rule by eye whether it earns its cost.

    Proves · what Jev adds over the rules alone, and at what speed

    Estimate: 1 session. The whole proof of concept: 11 to 15 sessions, estimated.

After the proof of concept · not estimated

  • The bank's half. The real identity and entitlement service, real data connectors, a security review. Not ours alone.
  • Widen the catalogue. When-rules for the rest of the 137 parts, a part family at a time.
  • More signals. Email and Teams, once consent and privacy are settled.
  • More roads. A2A and AG-UI, if a host needs them.

The five gaps

  1. No way to describe a screen as data.

    Our parts are finished pages, not parts you can fill. The one exception is the chart renderer, which already draws from a data description.

    Closes with: the A2UI catalogue and the renderer, steps one and two.

  2. No way to plug live data in.

    We name 23 shapes of data, but nothing maps a real source onto a part.

    Closes with: A2UI's data model and bindings, mapped to our 23 shapes. Mock data first.

  3. The gates are fast but tied to files.

    They read files from the repo and write reports to disk. The render check needs a browser, and its time is unmeasured.

    Closes with: gates as a service, step three.

  4. Choosing is by keyword and role, not a plan.

    For a treasury cash view the chooser offered a candlestick chart. It returns candidates, not a layout.

    Closes with: the rules' evaluator, step four, with Jev as an optional ranker, step six.

  5. Nothing knows about the person.

    Entitlements, context, consent and audit, the whole vehicle half of June's note, have nothing in the repo yet.

    Closes with: the four best-guess roles in the proof of concept; the bank's identity service after it.

Risks and controls for a regulated bank

In agent screens, the screen itself is part of the attack surface.

RiskWhat it looks likeThe control
A steered agentA document or message tells the agent to show something else, or act for someone else.[36][37]The agent can only pick from the catalogue. Entitlements are checked at the tool, never taken from the agent. The gates refuse what breaks the rules.

What you decide

Six calls. Each comes with a recommendation.

  1. The route.

    Recommend route B: Apollo as an A2UI catalogue over MCP, with MCP Apps as the fallback shell. It reaches every major host today, and we own only what is ours.

  2. The proof of concept, as scoped.

    Recommend it as written: the landing dashboard, about 15 parts, mock data, four best-guess roles, show and prepare only. It proves the whole loop without touching anything real.

  3. Jev in the GenUI layer: yes, no or later.

    Recommend yes for the proof of concept, as a ranker behind a switch that is off by default. You get the side-by-side evidence without making the layer depend on it.

  4. The name.

    Apollo-MCP names the pipe, and the pipe may change: A2UI also travels over A2A and AG-UI. Plain alternatives: Apollo Live, Apollo Wire, Apollo Floor.

    Recommend Apollo Live, because it says what it does and outlasts any one standard.

  5. Screens that move money.

    Recommend data-only in the target, never raw HTML, with confirmation kept in the bank's own flow. The proof of concept moves no money at all.

  6. When to start.

    Recommend starting the proof of concept now. Its first four steps pay back at design time, and June called run-time gates "the one shared investment".
