The claim this page tests

What the published evidence does — and does not — say about "six months becomes six weeks"

Dave's estimate, said as an estimate (s268-D8): "a six month project may become six weeks or even less if we use AI in research and user testing." Six months to six weeks is a 77% reduction in elapsed delivery time — a 4.3× compression. No published study measures anything that size on a whole project. The largest controlled effect on a whole task is 55.8%. The largest bank-scale effect anyone has published is 15% — and it is HSBC's own.

Read the 77% arithmetic before the room does. 26 weeks → 6 weeks requires every phase to compress, including the phases no study touches: procurement, stakeholder alignment, compliance sign-off, recruiting real participants, and waiting for a change window. The evidence below compresses the making and the analysing. It says nothing about the waiting.

The three a Chief AI Officer has certainly already read

A CAIO at a G-SIB spends his week being pitched AI productivity numbers. These three are the ones that survive his filter, and two of them cut against the claim. Name them before he does — quoting the evidence against yourself is the fastest way to be believed on the rest.

1 · His own bank's number

15%, more than 20,000 developers

It is on hsbc.com. He either signed it off or was in the room when it was. Never state a delivery claim that implicitly calls his own published figure timid. The safe move is to adopt it: "your published number is 15% on coding time — we are not arguing with it; we are arguing about the other 85% of the project, which is the part that isn't coding."

hsbc.com/who-we-are/hsbc-and-digital/hsbc-and-ai/transforming-hsbc-with-ai

2 · The study everyone quotes at AI optimists

METR: 19% slower — and the 2026 walk-back

If you claim big acceleration without naming METR, he will name it for you. Say it first, and say the whole thing: the 2025 RCT found experts 19% slower on their own mature repos while believing they were 20% faster; METR's own February 2026 follow-up estimates a modest speed-up (−18% for returning devs) but calls its own data unreliable because devs now refuse to work without AI.

metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/ · metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/

3 · The one that makes Apollo's case

DORA 2025: AI amplifies what is already there

~5,000 respondents. 90% use AI; throughput correlates positively with adoption; stability correlates negatively; and internal-platform quality is the thing that determines whether AI pays. This is the report that says a governed component library is the precondition, not the garnish — and it is Google's, not ours.

cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report

Runner-up, if he is a reader: Dell'Acqua et al.'s "jagged frontier" (758 BCG consultants, +12.2% tasks at −25.1% time, but 19 points worse outside the frontier). It is the standard citation for "AI helps, until it confidently doesn't" — the single best framing for why every Apollo output passes a gate.

The honest bound

What this evidence does not support saying

1

"Six months becomes six weeks" — as a benchmark

No published study measures a 77% compression of an end-to-end delivery. The largest controlled effect on a single, self-contained task is 55.8%; the largest enterprise-realistic RCT is ~21%; NN/g's own estimate for AI-assisted UX work is +33%. Even multiplying the two independent effects that are measured — 47% from the parts bin and ~55% from the assistant — compresses the making by roughly 76%, and the making is not the whole project.

Sayable: it is Dave's estimate, offered as an estimate (s268-D8), and the room is told which half is measured and which is predicted. Not sayable: "the research shows six months becomes six weeks."

2

"AI compresses user research"

It compresses the analysis of research — 492 minutes to 15 in the one controlled comparison found. It does not compress fieldwork: NN/g's own line is that a one-hour customer visit consumes an hour. And the shortcut that would compress it — synthetic participants — has been tested by NN/g against three of its own real studies and found sycophantic and shallow, with an explicit instruction not to substitute it for real users.

Sayable: "we cut the analysis, not the interviews." Not sayable: anything involving synthetic users in front of a bank that answers to the FCA on customer outcomes.

3

"Faster, with no bill"

Three independent findings say speed buys instability. DORA 2025: AI adoption correlates negatively with delivery stability across ~5,000 respondents. GitClear: 8× growth in duplicated blocks across 211 million changed lines, with reuse falling below 10%. Dell'Acqua et al.: 19 percentage points worse on tasks outside AI's frontier. And METR's 2025 RCT is the standing proof that a confident practitioner's sense of speed-up can be wrong by 39 points.

Sayable: "the gates are the reason the speed is safe, and reuse rate is the metric that proves it." Not sayable: a speed number without a quality number beside it.

The defensible sentence for beat 2: "The published range for AI on software delivery runs from 19% slower to 56% faster depending on how hard and how familiar the work is — your own bank publishes 15% on coding time. What none of those studies had is a governed parts bin: the one controlled test of that, pre-AI, cut build time 47% on its own. Our claim is about stacking the two, and here are the five things we would measure to find out whether it holds."
