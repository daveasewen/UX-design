The story in one line

At three dozen parts the machine invented, because parts were missing. At a hundred and thirty‑seven it copied, because judgement was missing. Inventory was necessary. It was not sufficient. That is why Apollo has a brain.

Seven beats

The arc, in the order the room hears it.

  1. The parts bin.

    An assembly line is only as fast as its parts bin. Ours is a bin of parts nobody knows end to end — so every build is slow, no two come out the same, and quality is whoever reached in. Three problems, one picture.

  2. We tried to automate it. Underwhelming.

    Not the rules — the bin. Where the library was silent the machine invented: colours, chart bars, easings, account numbers. And we did the same thing by hand.

  3. So we built the inventory.

    About three dozen components to a hundred and thirty‑seven, each one in code, named, levelled, gated. Coverage removes the reason to invent.

    Show · the library

  4. Then it stopped inventing and started copying.

    Parts without judgement ship the specimen. So we built the engineer: everything a designer knows — standards, accessibility, rulings, governance, usage, theory — as one graph you can ask why.

    Show · the explorer

  5. And an inspector.

    A designer who cannot be told no is not one. Gates that withhold "done"; evals that say whether this run beat the last.

  6. The build.

    One frozen prompt, cold, unedited. Then the inspector checks it in front of the room.

    Show · live, with the recording as fallback

  7. The ask.

    Help and support to develop it further. Not a budget line, not a KPI. Research is named as owed — another group's work, with Dave alongside.

What she is made of

Three parts. The analogy names them.

The assembly line supplies the words: what is in the bin, who builds from it, who checks the work. "Robot" is implied by the line and need not be said; "designer" stays as the word for the whole.

The inventory

Everything she builds with — tokens, components in code, patterns, assets. Catalogued, levelled, gated.

The engineer

Everything she knows — the standards, the rules, the rulings, the governance, the usage, the theory. One graph you can ask why.

The inspector

Everything she checks — gates that can withhold "done", evals that say whether this run was better than the last.

Behind beats two to four

The record dates the turn.

18 June
32 reviewed components. The count stays at 32 for thirteen days.
30 June
The realisation. One brief, run four ways. The record's own line: "the canon was silent in exactly the places it had to invent." Invented charcoals, chart bars, easings; a cold run fabricated account numbers and status chips.
1 July
The decision, in the target document: "when a part is missing, the model invents … coverage removes the reason to invent." Library at ~38.
7 July
The measurement that settled it. The governed run: zero invented colours, 26 canon components — and flat. The unconstrained run: 56 invented hex colours, 219 invented local variables — and it read better. Rule‑adherence was already saturated. It was never a rules problem.
20 August
92 to 136 components in one day. 137 today, plus 8 foundations, 111 stable and 34 beta, all read from the showroom index.
5 September
The bookend. With the bin full, a builder with nothing to decide with ships the specimen — 470 rules, none about composition. The library dictates; judgement is the next box.

His word, and the measured

Two halves of one sentence, told differently.

The machine inventing at the gaps is measured. Thirteen of forty snippets carried their own button recipe; seven copied the press physics; four had drifted. Dated, filed, quotable.

The designers inventing at the gaps is Dave's observation. The record holds the mechanism — the "Gaps and edits" Figma branch with its duplicate frames and a page flagging its own divergence — but no dated finding says designers invented components. Told first‑person it is the stronger line: I watched our designers do exactly what the machine did.

The number. The repo never says 36. It says 32, then about 38, then 137. For the room: about three dozen, now a hundred and thirty‑seven.
