The bento, at the parameters you ruled

Six columns, 320px row unit, dense packing, outer padding 0 — shared by all four themes. The gutter is the only thing that moves between themes: 0 in mono and supercharge, 24 in legacy and console. Switch the theme above and watch the space between tiles open and close while nothing else does.

Photography — 15 committed derivatives, spans derived

Nothing here is arranged by eye. Every derivative is 1600×1067 (1.50:1), which is below the 2.60:1 two-column threshold, so the aspect rule alone would draw a plain grid. The mixed emphasis you can see is the rhythm: every 6th tile from tile 1 is promoted to 2×2. Position, not preference. The aspect thresholds are photography-only — card spans below are authored.

This wall has also been through the squaring pass (below): 4 of its 15 tiles were re-spanned so the bottom edge is straight at every band. It fills exactly 5 / 10 / 15 / 15 rows at 6, 3, 2 and 1 columns.

A portrait photograph gets a taller compartment

You asked whether a portrait could default to a taller compartment. It already does, and you ruled it — s217-D2 carries a two-row threshold at taller than 1:1.15. Nothing was decided to make this wall; three portrait derivatives were minted from the library and the derivation put them where you can see them. Portrait tiles are two rows tall, landscape tiles one.

Measured, not asserted. 3 of these 6 photographs are portrait (1065×1600, 1106×1600, 1067×1600), and each resolves to data-r="2". The threshold is read from tokens/layout.json, so it is the same number the stylesheet comment quotes.

No orphaned compartments

Ruled — s217-D3 (Q1)

Your second question at #217: “Is there a way to avoid orphaned compartments so the bento is always cohesively rectangular?” Yes, and you ratified it — s217-D3: “SQUARING PASS RATIFIED for dashboard and brochureware roles”, with gallery exempted in the same breath. It has to be decided when the wall is built, not patched afterwards: CSS can back-fill holes but it cannot resize a tile, so no stylesheet and no script can straighten that bottom edge. The mechanism below is ruled. What is still yours is narrower, and it is stated underneath rather than implied by a badge over the whole thing.

How it works, in one breath. The generator simulates the browser’s own dense placement, asks whether every cell down to the last row is filled, and if not re-spans the last few tiles until it is — testing every column count the responsive bands produce, not just the wide one. A wall that is square at six columns and ragged at three is the same defect, later. Photographs get a say: laying a portrait on its side to square a wall costs more than nudging a card, so the pass avoids it. It is deterministic — the same tiles always give the same wall — and when a wall genuinely cannot be a rectangle at every band (three tiles at six columns cannot) it says so by name rather than shipping a ragged edge quietly.

A bento of bentos

Your example, built: three bentos at 1px gutter inside one outer bento at 40px. Each level carries its own parameter set — the inner walls are not inheriting the outer’s tuning, and the outer is not inheriting the theme’s. No JavaScript, no new classes: an inner bento is a tile that is also a bento.

What is canon here, and what is this page

Canon: the container, the grid, the span vocabulary, the bands and the radius model — all of it generated into knowledge/canon/canon.css by canon/gen_canon_bento.py from tokens/layout.json (layout/bento). The gutter override lives in the legacy and console override sets. This page declares no grid, no gap and no bento radius of its own — it styles tile content and its own chrome, and nothing else.
